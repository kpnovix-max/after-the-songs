After the Songs

Chapter Nine

The Children of the Island

The water at Nīlathurai was never content to remain one colour.

At dawn it was pearl-grey beneath the mist, as if the sea had not yet decided whether to wake. By midmorning it became green where the boats were moored, pale glass over sand, then deepened suddenly into a blue so clear and improbable that strangers often stood upon the shore for several moments before trusting their own eyes. In the hottest part of the day the sunlight struck the shallows until they seemed lit from underneath. By evening the edges of the harbour turned gold. At sunset the outer water caught fire and burned gently until the dark put it out.

Analā had lived long enough beside that shore to stop being astonished by it every morning.

She had not lived long enough to stop loving it.

The beach curved eastward in a long quiet sweep, white where the sand dried, honey-coloured where the tide still held it, and broken here and there by dark rocks polished smooth by years of water. Fishing catamarans rested above the tide line like sleeping animals. Nets lay spread on stakes to dry, their knots shining with salt. Palm trees leaned inland, all in one direction, as though persuaded by a wind that had spent many years arguing with them. Farther up, on a rise beyond the line of houses, a shrine painted in faded red watched over the harbour. Someone had left jasmine there at dawn. The flowers were already beginning to brown at the edges.

A woman carrying a basket of prawns called out to Analā as she passed.

“You're late.”

“I'm on time,” Analā said.

“You are on your own time.”

“That is still a kind of time.”

The woman clicked her tongue, satisfied by the answer, and went on.

A boy running barefoot with a coil of rope over his shoulder nearly collided with her, swerved at the last moment, shouted an apology without slowing and vanished between two upturned boats. From one of the houses came the smell of tamarind and frying chillies. From another came the dry rattling sound of shells being sorted. Somewhere behind her a man had begun to sing while scraping fish. The song wandered in and out of tune and did not seem to care.

Analā walked slowly along the beach path towards the harbour.

She carried a bundle of linen under one arm and a curved wooden needle tucked into the knot of her sash. A piece of sailcloth was slung over her shoulder. There was tar beneath one fingernail that she had not been able to scrape away that morning, and a faint white scar across the side of her left hand where a fish hook had once gone in too deep. Her feet were bare. The hems of her skirt were dark with seawater. The sun had browned her skin in places that no palace life would ever have touched. The wind had made her hair more stubborn than it had been when she was fifteen. She did not mind.

People here knew her as the woman who repaired sailcloth neatly, mended nets firmly, and argued about payment with more calm than most fishermen thought proper in a person so young.

No one in Nīlathurai called her princess.

The village lay a little east of the old mainland settlement of Uchipuli, on the northern shore of a shallow sea that separated the southern lands from Lanka. Beyond the narrow coastal plain were groves of palmyra, salt pans and scrubland where goats wandered between thorn trees. To the east lay the busier fishing settlements near the long sandbanks and reefs. To the south, beyond the water, was the island kingdom from which Analā had come.

Nīlathurai itself was neither large nor important. Merchants passed through it because the harbour was sheltered and the water was good. Fishermen stayed because they knew the shoals better than strangers did. The local headman collected dues for a mainland ruler whose principal interest in the village was that its boats continued arriving with fish, salt and occasional taxable cargo. Few people could remember the last time that ruler had visited. Fewer still wished him to correct the omission.

Analā preferred it that way.

The village had no palace.

No great avenue.

No painted gates announcing a king's benevolence.

There was a meeting hall with a roof that leaked during the monsoon and a harbour clerk who was capable of losing documents while sitting directly upon them.

The sea, however, was magnificent.

Beyond the curve of the beach a low rocky headland rose from the coast, its upper slopes covered in scrub and coarse grass. In the morning haze it seemed taller than it was. Its dark face caught the sunlight while the sea beneath remained in shadow.

Analā slowed when she saw it.

It was not the same cliff.

The one in Lanka had been larger, steeper, and darker above the water. The sea had broken against its base with a violence that this sheltered coast seldom knew. There had been a road cut through the upper rock, a platform where men could stand and look out over the western harbour, and a wooden structure she had not understood when she first saw it from the evacuation boats.

The resemblance was enough.

Seven years disappeared.

She was fifteen again, walking towards the harbour with a bundle in her arms and the sound of a slap still alive in her ears.

She had not intended to slap the officer.

That was the first thing she remembered.

She had intended to walk past him. She had intended to let the man gather his broken lamps. She had intended, after all the questions she had asked her father and all the answers she had received, to leave Lanka quietly.

Then the officer had lifted his hand towards her face.

She had struck him.

Now, as she hurried through the narrowing streets, the memory of it seemed almost impossible. She could still feel the roughness of his cheek against her palm. She could still see the instant of surprise before his hand went towards his sword.

She had slapped an officer.

An officer who might have recognised her.

An officer who served the victorious army.

An officer who, by now, might be standing before her father's guards insisting that the king's daughter had assaulted him.

Her stomach tightened.

She looked over her shoulder.

Nobody appeared to be following.

That did not help very much.

The late sun lay along the western roofs. Shopkeepers were pulling shutters across their stalls. Women gathered the last of their vegetables into baskets. Two men carried a broken wooden wheel between them, still arguing over whose fault it was. The city continued its ordinary business with such indifference that Analā felt briefly insulted by it.

She had just walked away from her father.

She had struck a soldier.

She was leaving the kingdom.

And a woman beside the road was complaining that her neighbour had borrowed a cooking pot and returned it with a crack.

Analā almost laughed.

Instead she began walking faster.

At the next intersection she heard men shouting.

Her breath caught.

She stepped behind a cart loaded with sacks of grain and waited.

The shouting came closer.

A man appeared carrying a coil of rope, followed by another who was calling him a thief.

They passed without looking at her.

Analā remained behind the cart long after they had gone.

Only then did she notice that her hands were trembling.

It was an irritating discovery.

She had managed to speak to her father without trembling. She had asked him questions she had been afraid to ask for more than a year. She had listened to answers that wounded her and remained standing. She had even found the courage to embrace him.

Yet now she was frightened by two men arguing about rope.

She pressed her fingers against the side of the cart until they steadied.

Then she went on.

At the harbour, lamps were beginning to appear along the quay.

The larger naval vessels lay farther out, their dark hulls drawn up beyond the shallow water. Nearer the landing steps, fishing boats were returning with their evening catch. Men carried baskets of silver fish towards the market. A few traders were still loading coastal craft before the tide turned.

Analā knew enough about boats to understand that she needed to leave before the harbour settled for the night.

She also knew very little about the business of arranging passage.

On Sēnan's island, one asked a boatman where he was going and whether he had room. If he liked you, he might take you. If he disliked you, Mēni would usually find out why and correct the matter before supper.

Lanka's harbour was not Sēnan's island.

Here there were fees, sailing lists, cargo permits and men who seemed personally offended by questions.

Analā approached a boat preparing to leave for the southern island.

The sight of its familiar destination almost undid her.

For one foolish moment she imagined herself aboard it, travelling directly to the harbour she knew, with Sēnan waiting somewhere near the storehouse and Ilā running down the path before anyone had finished tying the boat.

She wanted that.

She wanted Mēni's kitchen.

She wanted the sound of Ilā talking too quickly.

She wanted to be somewhere nobody would ask her to explain what had happened in Lanka.

But she also knew what would happen if she went there.

People would ask questions.

They would expect her to return to the household that had saved her. They would welcome her. They would love her.

And before long, somebody would suggest that she should remain.

Her father would certainly expect her to go there.

That thought gave her an idea.

It was not a very good idea.

At fifteen, she thought it was excellent.

The boatman was sitting beside a low table while a clerk entered passengers' names into a palm-leaf register.

“Where are you travelling?” he asked without looking up.

“To the southern island.”

“Name?”

“Analā.”

He looked up.

“Family?”

She hesitated.

For a moment she imagined giving her father's name and watching the man leap to his feet.

Instead she said, “I came from the island. I'm returning.”

He studied her.

Then he shrugged.

“Passage is three copper pieces. We leave when the cargo is ready.”

She paid.

The clerk scratched her name into the preliminary sailing list.

Analā watched the marks appear.

There it was.

A small fact, written by a man who knew nothing about her.

Analā. Southern island. Evening departure.

If her father sent soldiers to the harbour, they would ask which vessels had carried passengers away.

Someone would show them the register.

They would read her name.

They would follow the obvious destination.

Perhaps they would send a message to Sēnan.

Perhaps they would go themselves.

Either way, they would be looking for her somewhere she did not intend to be.

She felt a sudden, ridiculous pleasure in having thought of it.

Then she remembered that the same soldiers might frighten Ilā and Mēni.

The pleasure disappeared.

For several moments she stood beside the boat, unable to decide what to do.

She could still abandon the plan.

She could go back to her father.

She could tell him she had changed her mind, or that she needed time, or that she was frightened.

He would take her back.

Of that she had almost no doubt.

She could imagine his face when she appeared at the palace gates. The relief he would try to conceal. The careful way he would ask whether she wished to eat.

The image hurt so unexpectedly that she had to look away.

She placed her bundle aboard the island-bound vessel.

Then she returned to the quay.

The clerk was arguing with a porter about a basket of dried fish. The boatman had gone ashore to collect another passenger.

Analā waited until the porter carried the basket away.

Then she stepped aboard, retrieved her bundle and slipped between two stacks of cargo on the quay.

Nobody stopped her.

Nobody asked where she was going.

She had expected deception to require more ceremony.

At the far end of the landing place a small coastal vessel was preparing to depart before darkness. It was carrying rope, grain and a few passengers towards the northwestern ports of Lanka.

Analā approached the man supervising its loading.

“Where are you going?”

He named two harbours she knew only from trade maps.

“Will you take a passenger?”

“How much can you pay?”

She told him what remained after the first boatman's fare.

He looked at the coins.

Then at her bundle.

Then at her face.

“You will sleep beside the cargo,” he said.

“I don't need a bed.”

“There isn't one.”

“I understood.”

He took the money.

“Get aboard.”

Analā climbed down into the boat.

Only when she had settled behind a stack of grain sacks did she realise that she had left her name on one vessel and placed herself aboard another.

It was done.

She waited for triumph.

Instead she felt sick.

The harbour was still close enough to swim to.

If her father sent men now, they might find her before the boat moved.

If the officer from the square had reported the slap, perhaps the harbour gates would be closed.

If somebody recognised her, perhaps she would be taken back to the palace.

The possibilities multiplied until she could scarcely breathe.

She kept her head lowered.

A sailor shouted for the final rope.

Another answered.

The mooring line fell into the water.

The boat moved.

Analā stared at the widening strip of dark water between the hull and the quay.

She was leaving.

The thought seemed astonishingly simple.

Then somebody shouted behind her.

“Stop!”

She ducked so quickly that her forehead struck a wooden spar.

The boatman swore.

“Watch where you're putting your head.”

Analā pressed herself against the sacks.

The voice shouted again.

She could hear running feet on the quay.

For several terrible moments she believed the officer had found her. She imagined soldiers boarding the boat, demanding names, turning faces towards the last of the evening light.

Then the boatman leaned over the side and shouted back.

“You'll get your baskets tomorrow!”

“Those baskets belong to me!”

“Then you should have loaded them yesterday!”

The men exchanged several further opinions about baskets, mothers and professional honesty.

The vessel continued moving.

Analā remained behind the sacks.

Her knees were shaking.

She pressed both hands against them.

A laugh escaped her.

It was a small, ugly sound.

Then, without warning, she began to cry.

She tried to stop. She was furious with herself for crying. She had not cried when she left her father's room. She had not cried when she slapped the officer.

Now she was crying because a man wanted his baskets.

She hid her face in her bundle.

Nobody came to comfort her.

Nobody knew there was anything to comfort.

The boat sailed on.

The western harbour receded slowly beneath the evening sky.

Lanka looked beautiful from the water.

That offended her too.

The city had been broken, burned and occupied. Her uncle was dead. Her mother was dead. Her brother Arun had been buried in a grave she had visited only recently. The palace had become her father's palace, and men who claimed to have liberated the country shouted at its people in the streets.

Yet from the sea, as the first lamps appeared on the rising slopes, the city looked almost peaceful.

She could distinguish the pale roofs of the upper houses, the long harbour walls, the towers repaired after the siege. Above them the sky changed from amber to violet.

And then the cliff came into view.

The vessel was following the western coast northwards, keeping close enough to the shore to avoid the stronger offshore current.

For the first time Analā saw the great cliff from the water in fading daylight.

Not as a distant black shape against a burning sky.

Not as the last piece of Lanka visible through smoke.

She saw the cliff itself.

The dark stone rose steeply from the sea, its face cut by narrow ledges where seabirds gathered. Coarse grass and scrub bent along the upper edge. A road climbed behind the rock towards the old western defences.

Near the summit she could distinguish a platform.

Below it, a line of weathered timber projected towards the sea.

The remains of the launching rail.

She recognised it immediately.

On the night of the evacuation she had seen something rise from that place.

A strange pale frame, its wings opening against the firelit sky. Lamps flashing beneath it. Boats turning towards the signals. Men shouting instructions that the children could not hear clearly through the smoke.

And her uncle standing somewhere above them.

She had lifted her hand.

She had waved.

She had not known whether he could see her.

Now the distance was smaller.

She could see individual stones in the cliff face. She could see where sections of timber had fallen away. She could see a narrow strip of path descending towards the water.

She searched the platform as though a man might still be standing there.

There was nobody.

The evening wind moved through the scrub.

A bird lifted from the ledge and circled towards the sea.

Analā remembered the first evacuation boat rocking beneath her, the smell of smoke, the voices of frightened children and the terrible excitement of escaping.

She remembered searching for Arun among the faces aboard.

She remembered the wooden horse another boy had carried, and the brief hope that had ended when she saw both its ears.

She remembered her mother telling her she would come.

And she remembered her uncle.

He had saved the children.

She knew that much.

But what had happened after their boats disappeared into darkness?

Had he watched until the last vessel was safe?

Had he returned to the palace?

Had he known he was going to die?

Had he been frightened?

The last question stayed with her.

She had never thought of him as frightened when she was younger.

Not because he was cruel or impossible to approach. Quite the opposite. He laughed easily. He made mistakes. He complained about doors that would not open properly and servants who moved his instruments without asking.

But he had always seemed to know what to do next.

Now, looking at the empty platform, she wondered whether that had been true.

The boat passed beneath the outer face of the cliff.

For a few moments the summit disappeared above her.

She tilted her head back and watched the dark rock rise almost vertically from the water.

Somewhere up there, she thought, her uncle had stood while the children sailed away.

She wanted to ask him why he had stayed.

She wanted to know whether he had wished to leave.

She wanted to know if he had seen her wave.

The questions remained with the cliff.

The boat sailed north.

The first night was the easiest.

That was something she understood only much later.

She had paid for passage. She had somewhere to sleep, however uncomfortable. The boatmen had work to do and little interest in her. Nobody expected her to earn money, make decisions or explain her presence beyond the destination she had named.

She was frightened, but she was moving.

Movement felt enough.

At dawn they reached the first northwestern fishing harbour.

Analā stepped ashore with her bundle and discovered that she had no useful idea what to do next.

The boatman had told her that another vessel would be leaving for the next port in two days.

Two days required food.

Two days required somewhere to sleep.

She had not calculated either very carefully.

She found work carrying baskets between a fish market and a drying yard. The labour was unpleasant, the payment small, and by evening her shoulders ached so badly that lifting her bundle made her gasp.

She slept in a storage shed after persuading the owner to let her occupy a corner.

The following morning she woke to the sound of men outside discussing the royal household.

One mentioned that the king's daughter had returned.

Another said she had disappeared again.

Analā lay still.

The men argued about whether the story was true.

One claimed that the girl had been taken away for her own protection. Another said she had quarrelled with the king. A third said the whole account was nonsense because no daughter of Vibhishana would be travelling without an escort.

Analā waited until their voices faded before she moved.

That afternoon she left the harbour.

For the next several weeks she moved whenever staying began to feel dangerous.

She travelled from one fishing settlement to another along Lanka's northwestern coast.

At one harbour she stayed three nights.

At another she spent almost a week helping an elderly sailmaker repair storm-damaged canvas.

At a third she left before sunrise because a trader had asked too many questions about her accent and the work she had learned on the southern island.

Each move cost money.

Each new place required explanations.

She began using only her first name, then sometimes no name at all when a boatman was willing to take her fare without asking.

The girl who had thought herself clever for leaving a false passenger booking in Lanka discovered that escaping a search was not a single trick.

It was an exhausting habit.

She watched faces.

She avoided officials.

She learned which merchants travelled regularly between ports and might remember her.

She became suspicious of strangers who were merely curious and grateful to strangers who were merely indifferent.

And she began to dislike herself for both.

Once, in a harbour near the northern shallows, she saw two men wearing the colours of Lanka's royal service.

They were buying supplies.

There was no reason to believe they were looking for her.

Analā nevertheless abandoned a half-finished repair, left the money she was owed and spent the afternoon hiding behind a storehouse.

She was ashamed afterwards.

She was also alive and still free.

That was becoming an increasingly complicated achievement.

By the time she crossed the narrow waters towards the southern Indian mainland, she had travelled through four harbours, slept in places she would once have refused to enter, lost more money than she had earned and learned to fear the sound of footsteps approaching from behind.

She had also begun to understand something about distance.

The sea between Lanka and the mainland was not particularly wide in places.

But crossing it changed the nature of her disappearance.

A soldier carrying her father's authority could command a Lankan harbour clerk.

He could inspect records, question officers and demand a passenger list.

On the mainland, he would first have to find the right town, persuade the right official and discover that the young woman he wanted had ever been there.

And even that assumed he knew she had crossed.

Analā did not mistake this for perfect safety.

It was simply a place where being found might require more trouble than anyone wished to undertake.

At her first mainland landing she heard people speaking a dialect she could mostly understand but not always answer quickly. She saw unfamiliar merchants wearing familiar clothes, fishermen whose boats were shaped differently from those on Sēnan's island, and women selling food beneath woven shelters that smelled of salt, smoke and fresh coconut.

She bought something hot to eat.

It was the first proper meal she had paid for in several days.

She ate too quickly and burned her tongue.

Then she sat beside the harbour wall with her bundle in her lap and tried to decide where to go.

A boatman had mentioned a village a little east of Uchipuli.

Nīlathurai.

He said the harbour was small but the fishing was good. Boats needed mending. Sails needed patching. Nobody there would care much where a girl had been born if she could do useful work.

Analā liked the sound of that.

She did not know how much labour lay between knowing how to repair a net and persuading someone to pay a fair price for repairing one.

She did not know how quickly money disappeared when there was nobody else to buy supper.

She did not know which men were dangerous, which women were kind, or how lonely a room could become when there was no one waiting for her outside it.

She thought she understood hardship because she had survived the war.

Perhaps she did understand one kind.

She was about to learn several others.

Seven years later, Analā stood upon the beach at Nīlathurai and smiled at the memory.

Not at the fear.

Not at the girl who had cried behind the grain sacks.

But at the extraordinary confidence with which that girl had once believed a false passenger booking and three copper pieces could defeat the combined intelligence of a kingdom.

It had been a remarkably poor plan.

She had survived it partly through luck, partly through stubbornness, and partly because she had never discovered whether anyone had seriously tried to follow her.

Perhaps her father had sent soldiers.

Perhaps they had gone to Sēnan's island and returned with nothing.

Perhaps he had discovered her deception almost immediately.

Perhaps, after searching long enough to know that she was alive, he had simply allowed her to remain where she wished.

She would never know unless she asked him.

And she had not asked.

The thought no longer frightened her as it once had.

The tide moved over her feet.

Far down the shore, a fisherman called to another, and the answer travelled back across the water.

Analā adjusted the sailcloth upon her shoulder and resumed walking towards the harbour.

A man was sitting beside an overturned canoe, struggling with a net whose knots had become an increasingly personal insult to him.

He looked up as she approached.

“Tell me if this knot is ugly.”

Analā took the edge of the mesh and examined it.

Then she pulled one length free with a sharp movement of her fingers.

“It is not ugly,” she said. “It is foolish.”

The man watched the ruined knot dangle from her hand.

“It would have held.”

“It would have held until it did not.”

“That describes several of my cousins and one marriage.”

Analā handed the net back.

“Then treat this with greater seriousness than your cousins.”

He grunted, which in Pakkan's language meant gratitude.

She walked on.

There had been a time when she could not have spoken to a fisherman like that without sounding either frightened or proud.

Now the speech came easily.

It belonged to the life she had built.

That life had not been kind at first.

Analā remembered arriving in Nīlathurai with the extraordinary confidence of someone who had survived a war, escaped a king and crossed a sea, and had therefore concluded that ordinary life could not possibly have anything important left to teach her.

She was fifteen.

She could mend a net, splice a rope, patch sailcloth and tell by the colour of the water whether a reef lay beneath it. She could clean fish without making a face, carry water without spilling half of it, and cook rice well enough that Mēni had once stopped supervising her.

She had earned money on Sēnan's island.

Not much, certainly. A few copper pieces for repairing a torn net. A little more for helping prepare rope for a merchant. Once she had earned enough in a week to buy Ilā a pair of silver-coloured shell earrings, and had felt so prosperous that she bought herself sweet cakes as well.

She thought she knew how ordinary people lived.

The first mistake she made in Nīlathurai was accepting work from a man who smiled too much.

His name was Vēlan. He owned three fishing boats and a storehouse near the eastern end of the harbour. He examined the stitching on her bundle, asked where she had learned to work with fibre, and appeared impressed when she answered.

“Island work,” he said. “Good. Those people know their ropes.”

Analā warmed to him immediately.

Someone who respected island craftsmanship was unlikely to be entirely unpleasant.

He showed her a large net that had torn along one edge and asked whether she could repair it.

She examined the damage.

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“Tomorrow afternoon.”

He laughed.

“Tomorrow? My own men have been looking at it for four days.”

“Then perhaps they should have tried repairing it.”

He laughed again.

She liked him.

They agreed on a price.

Analā worked through the afternoon and into the next morning, sitting beneath the storehouse awning with the mesh spread across her knees. Her fingers grew sore, but she was pleased by the result. The repaired section was almost impossible to distinguish from the rest.

When Vēlan returned, he pulled at the knots, nodded thoughtfully and said it was satisfactory.

Then he paid her less than half the agreed amount.

Analā stared at the coins.

“There should be more.”

“The net was smaller than I remembered.”

“It was the same net yesterday.”

“The damage was less.”

“I showed you the damage.”

He shrugged.

“You are young. Perhaps you misunderstood.”

She felt heat rising in her face.

“I did not misunderstand.”

Vēlan's expression changed.

Not dramatically.

The smile simply went away.

“Are you calling me a liar?”

Analā had heard that question before. In the palace, it was usually the beginning of a discussion about a man who had already decided to lie.

“Yes,” she said.

The silence that followed was unexpectedly large.

Two labourers near the doorway stopped sorting baskets.

Vēlan put the coins back upon the table.

“You can take what I offer,” he said, “or you can leave it.”

“That isn't fair.”

“No.”

He said it so comfortably that she had no answer.

She waited for something else to happen.

For one of the labourers to intervene.

For somebody to point out that an agreement had been made.

For Vēlan to become embarrassed by the obvious injustice of his own behaviour.

Nobody moved.

At last she took the coins.

She needed the coins, but it was the first time in her life that being right had accomplished absolutely nothing.

The next mistake cost her somewhere to sleep.

A widow near the fish market rented sleeping space in a room behind her house. Analā paid for three nights in advance, relieved to have found somewhere with a roof, a door and a water jar.

On the second evening the widow announced that her nephew had arrived.

“He needs the room.”

“I paid for three nights.”

“He is family.”

“Then you should have told me yesterday.”

“He was not here yesterday.”

Analā looked at the sleeping mat, the neatly folded cloth beside it and the small bundle containing everything she owned.

“Then return my money.”

The widow shook her head.

“You slept here.”

“For one night.”

“And used water.”

Analā began to argue.

The widow shouted louder.

A man appeared in the doorway and asked what the trouble was. He looked at Analā, then at her bundle, then at the empty space behind her.

“Where is your family?” he asked.

“Elsewhere.”

“Your husband?”

“I am not married.”

“Your father?”

Analā said nothing.

The man's manner changed.

He smiled.

“Then perhaps we can arrange something.”

She gathered her belongings.

Outside, the market was closing. The streets smelled of fish scales, wet earth and extinguished cooking fires. Her bundle suddenly seemed much heavier than it had that morning.

She walked until she found a storage shed where a merchant allowed travellers to sleep for a small payment.

The place had no proper door.

She chose a corner behind two large baskets, wrapped her bundle around her arm and lay down fully dressed.

Someone was coughing on the other side of the room.

A rat moved along the wall.

She had eaten nothing since midday.

She told herself that she was fortunate.

There was a roof.

She had coins.

She was free.

She repeated the words because she had nothing else to hold on to.

For the first time she imagined returning to Lanka.

Not as a possibility she would consider someday.

As a practical solution.

Her father would take her back.

She was almost certain of it.

There would be a bed.

Food.

A door that closed.

Someone would ask whether she needed anything, and the answer could be yes without requiring her to calculate its price.

She turned onto her side and pressed her face against her arm.

I could go tomorrow.

The thought was so comforting that it frightened her.

She had barely reached the mainland.

She had not yet endured a week in Nīlathurai.

And already she was imagining the palace.

She began to cry.

Not loudly.

She was too frightened of waking the strangers around her.

She lay there with tears making the cloth beneath her face damp and tried to remember exactly when she had become so certain that she knew what she was doing.

There were worse things than being cheated.

She discovered this on a night when she had stayed too late repairing rope for a coastal trader.

The man had paid her properly.

That was why she had remained.

It seemed, at the time, a sensible decision.

The market was almost empty when she left. A few lamps still burned beneath the awnings. Somewhere a child was calling for its mother. The sea breeze carried the smell of dried fish into the narrow lanes.

Analā had taken that route twice before.

She knew where it emerged.

She also knew that it was shorter than the main road.

Three men stood near a closed oil merchant's shop.

One stepped into her path.

“Where are you going?”

“Home.”

He looked at the bundle under her arm.

“Working late?”

“Yes.”

“Good girl.”

The words made her skin tighten.

She moved to pass him.

He moved too.

His companions laughed.

“Come,” he said. “We will walk with you.”

“No, thank you.”

“You should be grateful. Dangerous streets.”

She looked towards the main road.

It was visible beyond the far end of the lane, perhaps forty paces away.

One of the other men had already moved towards it.

Analā felt the first cold recognition that the conversation was not a conversation at all.

“I have people waiting,” she said.

“Then they can wait.”

She tried to step around him.

He caught the edge of her bundle.

Not her arm.

Only the cloth.

But he held it firmly enough that she could not move without letting go.

“Why are you frightened?” he asked.

His voice was almost gentle.

She could smell palm wine.

Analā had faced soldiers on Sēnan's island. She had stood before her father and asked questions that frightened her more than she could admit. She had slapped an officer whose hand was moving towards a sword.

None of those memories helped.

This was a narrow lane.

There were three men.

No one here knew her name.

The man tugged at the bundle.

She let it go.

For an instant he looked surprised.

Then she ran.

She heard footsteps behind her.

She did not turn.

She reached the main road and shouted for help.

A woman carrying a covered basket stopped.

Analā ran towards her.

The woman looked past Analā's shoulder and understood enough.

“Come here,” she said.

She pulled Analā beside her and shouted towards two men unloading a cart.

By the time the men looked up, the pursuers had disappeared into the lane.

The woman did not ask many questions.

She took Analā into a courtyard where several families were finishing supper and made her sit near the cooking fire.

Analā was shaking so violently that she could not hold the cup of water offered to her.

“Did they hurt you?” the woman asked.

Analā shook her head.

“Did they take anything?”

“My bundle.”

“Can it be replaced?”

“Yes.”

“Then drink.”

Analā obeyed.

She wanted to be grateful.

Instead she felt ashamed.

She had chosen the shortcut and stayed late because the work paid honestly. Why had she assumed that meant the streets would be safe? Worse, she had frozen when the man caught her bundle. She had imagined herself courageous; now she knew courage could disappear in the distance between two street lamps.

That night she did not sleep.

The following morning she went back to the lane with the woman and her grown son.

Her bundle lay beside a drain, wet and torn open.

Some coins were gone.

Her sewing needle remained.

She picked it up.

It was a ridiculous object to feel relieved about.

She held it so tightly that its curved point marked her palm.

For a while, Analā became frightened of almost everybody. A stranger asking her name, an official passing through the market, even a kind offer of work could set her wondering what was expected in return. She refused work inside unfamiliar houses, stopped going out after sunset and moved her sleeping place twice. Some evenings she was certain the whole village knew she had made a mistake.

More than men or poverty, she feared her own judgement. The girl who came here had thought kindness could be recognised by a smile and danger by a uniform. Fourteen months on the island had seemed like an education in the world; now she doubted she had understood much at all.

Sēnan had found her work with people he trusted; Mēni had fed her before she needed to ask, and Ilā had stood beside her when she was frightened. The islanders had made ordinary life safe for her. She had mistaken that safety for competence.

One evening she sat alone beside the harbour watching the fishing boats return.

She wanted Mēni.

Not the island.

Not even Ilā, whose presence would have brought more questions than she could bear that night.

She wanted Mēni to tell her what to do.

The desire made her feel so young that she became angry.

Then she cried again.

For several days she considered leaving Nīlathurai.

She could return to the island, or to Lanka.

She imagined arriving at her father's palace, tired and thinner, admitting that she had been foolish.

Would he say I told you so?

No.

That was the terrible thing.

He would probably embrace her.

He might not even ask why she had returned.

The certainty of his love hurt more than the possibility of his anger.

She missed him.

There were moments when she missed him so badly that the entire quarrel between them seemed distant and unreasonable.

Then she remembered Arun.

Her mother.

Her uncle.

The officer in the square.

And the knowledge that none of those things had ceased to matter merely because she was hungry and afraid.

Had she really believed the world outside the palace would be easy? She tried to remember. She had told Trijata that choices carried consequences, but choosing made a person free. At fifteen, saying it had felt like understanding it. Now the words returned almost as an accusation.

Choices have consequences.

She wanted to argue with Trijata that consequences were not meant to be so humiliating, so frightening or so expensive. Surely freedom ought to provide a little comfort in exchange for what it demanded. But Trijata had never promised comfort.

Analā stared at the water until the evening light disappeared.

Then she went back to her room.

She did not decide to stay forever. Another week was the only promise she could manage.

The fever came during the first monsoon.

For three days rain fell so heavily that the far side of the harbour disappeared. Water ran through the streets in brown streams. The roofs leaked. The fishing boats remained onshore, and the men who owned them became increasingly bad-tempered as the weather continued to prove indifferent to their complaints.

Analā had been working beneath a storehouse awning when she began to feel cold.

By evening her head ached.

By morning she could scarcely stand.

She lived then in a small rented room behind a rope merchant's house. It had a mat, a water jar, one narrow window and a wooden door with a bolt that worked only if lifted at the correct angle.

She could barely afford the room, but after the men in the lane she was willing to pay for a door that bolted.

The merchant's wife noticed her absence from the morning market.

She knocked.

Analā answered without opening the door.

“Are you ill?”

“No.”

“You sound ill.”

“I am sleeping.”

“Then you sleep like a dying goat.”

Analā would normally have laughed.

Instead she slid down against the door because her legs would no longer hold her.

The woman heard the movement.

Her name was Kamali.

She was the woman who sold spiced lentil cakes near the harbour, and whose husband owned the rope shop beside Analā's room. She had a gap between her front teeth, a voice that could silence three quarrelling boatmen, and a habit of tying her hair so tightly that her forehead always seemed a little astonished.

“Open the door,” she said.

Analā tried.

The bolt stuck.

Kamali swore at the bolt, then called her husband.

Between them they lifted the latch from outside.

Kamali stepped in, looked at Analā sitting on the floor and said, “You look ridiculous.”

Analā attempted to smile.

“I was trying to improve the room.”

“By dying in it?”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“Then you have made a poor start.”

Kamali knelt and touched her forehead.

The humour left her face.

She called for water.

Then she sent her husband to fetch the neighbourhood physician.

Analā tried to protest.

“I can't pay—”

“Did I ask?”

“The physician—”

“Will be paid.”

“By whom?”

Kamali looked at her.

“By a woman who is losing patience with you.”

The physician listened to her breathing, asked about the fever and instructed her to rest, drink and eat.

Analā tried to explain that she had work to finish.

He told her she could finish it when she was no longer attempting to collapse through the floor.

Kamali moved her into the front room of the house.

Analā resisted until she discovered that standing made her dizzy.

She allowed Kamali's husband to carry her bedding, Kamali to bring water, and finally allowed herself to be fed.

There were humiliations greater than accepting help, but in those days she found it difficult to remember what they were.

Kamali's house was small and crowded with ordinary things.

Copper cooking pots hung above the hearth. A child's wooden spinning top lay beneath a stool. There were jars of dried chillies, sacks of grain, clean cloths folded beside the doorway and a shelf near the inner wall where a small painted image stood between two lamps.

Rama.

Analā recognised him immediately.

The bow.

The posture.

The familiar blue body.

Fresh flowers had been placed before the image.

Kamali noticed her looking.

“My mother painted that,” she said.

Analā said nothing.

“She made the face too solemn. I have always thought he looks as though someone has told him the rice is finished.”

Despite the fever, Analā laughed.

Kamali looked pleased.

Then she took a fresh cloth, dipped it in cool water and placed it across Analā's forehead.

In the evenings Kamali lit the two small lamps and sat before the image for a few minutes.

Sometimes she sang.

Sometimes she merely folded her hands.

Once Analā woke from a feverish sleep to hear Kamali praying.

She was asking Rama to protect the people of the village.

The fishermen at sea.

Her husband's ageing mother.

A neighbour whose baby had been sick.

And the young woman sleeping in the next room.

Analā lay still.

She had heard Rama's name shouted by men who broke lamps beneath the feet of frightened workers.

She had heard it used to demand gratitude, obedience and submission.

Now she heard it spoken softly by a woman who had carried water to a stranger.

The words were the same.

The voice was not.

When Kamali came to check on her, Analā asked, “Do you think Rama listens to you?”

Kamali considered the question.

“I hope so.”

“And if he doesn't?”

“Then I will still bring you supper.”

It was such a simple answer that Analā could find no argument against it.

A few days later, when the fever had begun to leave her, she asked another question.

“Do you think everyone who fought against Ravana was right?”

Kamali looked up from the vegetables she was cutting.

“No.”

Analā was surprised.

“You worship Rama.”

“Yes.”

“Then how can you say that?”

Kamali set down the knife.

“Do you agree with everything your father does?”

The question struck too close to something Analā had been trying not to touch.

“No.”

“Do you love him?”

Analā looked towards the doorway.

After a long moment she said, “Yes.”

Kamali picked up the knife again.

“There you are.”

Analā thought about this for a while.

It did not resolve the war or explain the dead. But Kamali had never promised either. She had promised supper, and each evening she brought it.

There was another man in Nīlathurai who spoke kindly of Ravana.

His name was Mādhavan, a trader who bought dried fish, rope and occasionally medicinal herbs from the smaller coastal villages. He was well dressed, generous with compliments and fond of saying that the old Lankan king had been a great scholar.

When he learned that Analā had lived in Lanka, he became particularly interested in her.

“Your king understood learning,” he told her one afternoon. “Not like these northern men who think a sword can settle every question.”

Analā liked him, even while warning herself not to.

He offered her regular work repairing the ropes used to bind cargo.

He paid promptly for the first job.

He paid generously for the second.

On the third occasion he asked her to come to his warehouse after dusk because a shipment had to leave early.

Analā hesitated.

He smiled.

“Surely a girl from Lanka knows how to recognise a friend.”

The words made her uncomfortable.

He had praised Ravana, paid fairly and treated her respectfully. She went.

The warehouse stood beyond the busiest part of the market, where the streets narrowed between storage buildings and the evening lamps were fewer.

Mādhavan was waiting inside.

There was no cargo ready for repair.

Only two men sitting beside a table.

Analā stopped at the doorway.

“Where are the ropes?”

“They will be brought,” he said.

One of the men rose and moved towards the door behind her.

She looked at him.

Then at Mādhavan.

Something in her face must have changed, because his smile became impatient.

“Don't look so frightened,” he said.

“I was told there was work.”

“There may be.”

“What work?”

He did not answer immediately.

The other man closed the door.

Analā felt the room become smaller.

Mādhavan gestured towards a stool.

“Sit. We should discuss what a girl travelling alone can offer a generous employer.”

She did not understand every detail. She understood enough.

She thought of the men in the lane, the abandoned bundle and running until her lungs hurt.

This time she did not run.

There was nowhere obvious to run.

Instead she said, “My tools are outside.”

Mādhavan frowned.

“What?”

“I left my needle with the boatman. I can't repair the cargo without it.”

“You won't need it yet.”

“Then I should fetch it before he leaves.”

She spoke calmly.

She was astonished that her voice remained steady.

Mādhavan considered her.

Then he laughed.

“You are a practical girl.”

He told the man by the door to let her fetch her tools.

Analā stepped outside.

She walked without hurrying until she had turned the first corner. Then she ran towards the market, where women were still selling vegetables beneath the lamps.

She did not stop until she reached Kamali's stall.

Kamali looked up.

Analā tried to speak.

Nothing came.

Kamali put down the ladle.

“Sit,” she said.

Analā sat.

Kamali waited.

At last Analā told her.

Not every word.

Enough.

Kamali listened without interrupting.

Then she stood, wiped her hands and called to her husband.

Analā caught her wrist.

“Please don't go there.”

Kamali looked down at the hand holding her.

For a moment her face was terrible.

Then she sat again.

“All right,” she said. “Not tonight.”

Analā began to shake.

Kamali poured water into a cup and placed it before her.

“You are safe here,” she said.

Analā wanted to believe her.

She did.

But she had believed Vēlan.

She had believed the widow.

She had believed Mādhavan.

And she had believed herself.

That night she wondered whether she possessed any ability at all to recognise another human being.

The man who praised Ravana had frightened her.

The woman who worshipped Rama had saved her.

Her father had betrayed her uncle and still loved her.

Her uncle had abducted a woman and saved hundreds of children.

The categories in which she had arranged the world were becoming useless.

She had thought she was escaping other people's ideas about who she ought to be.

Now she was discovering how many ideas she had brought with her about who other people ought to be.

Recovery did not arrive as a revelation.

There was no morning when Analā woke, understood the world and became courageous again.

For a while she remained afraid.

She still looked over her shoulder in narrow streets.

She still found it difficult to work for unfamiliar men.

She still sometimes imagined going home.

But Kamali introduced her to a woman named Ponnī who repaired fishing nets with three other women in a courtyard near the shore.

Ponnī was not impressed by her knowledge.

She examined Analā's first repair and pulled it apart.

“This knot is wrong.”

“It held on the island.”

“Then the island has different fish.”

Analā tried again.

Ponnī pulled it apart again.

“Too loose.”

A third attempt.

“Too tight.”

Analā grew angry.

Ponnī waited.

Then she showed her how to work the knot.

Not quickly.

Not gently.

Properly.

For several weeks Analā worked beside the women.

She learned that there were several ways to join damaged mesh, that different nets needed different repairs, that a knot excellent for one kind of fishing could fail under another, and that experienced craftswomen recognised weak work long before it broke.

She also learned to ask about payment before beginning.

Ponnī insisted upon it.

“Your hands are not a charitable institution,” she said.

Analā smiled.

The older woman did not.

“I am serious.”

“I know.”

“No. You will know when you have spent twenty years asking men for money they promised you yesterday.”

The other women laughed.

Analā laughed with them.

It was the first time in months that laughter had not felt like something she was borrowing from a better day.

When a fisherman tried to underpay her for a repair, she did not accuse him of dishonesty and wait for justice to embarrass him.

She showed him the completed work.

She reminded him of the agreement.

Then she said that any further repairs would require payment in advance.

He complained.

She remained seated.

He paid.

It was not a great victory.

Nobody cheered.

The fisherman went away muttering that young women had become difficult.

Analā spent the coins on rice, lamp oil and a little jaggery.

She ate the jaggery slowly that evening, enjoying every piece.

The second year was not easy.

Neither was the third.

There were bad storms, lean fishing seasons and months when work was scarce.

Once a larger merchant failed to pay several workers and left the village before anyone could recover the debt.

Once Analā injured her hand and could not sew for nearly three weeks.

Once she trusted a traveller who promised to carry a letter to Ilā and later discovered he had used the folded paper to wrap dried herbs.

That last betrayal hurt disproportionately.

She had hesitated for weeks before writing. A letter could reveal where she was, and she knew her father might still be looking for her. But she wanted someone who loved her to know she was alive. She had almost written to him.

Instead, she chose Ilā.

She trusted her friend to keep a secret, even from Sēnan and Mēni if necessary. It was a risk. But she had grown tired of treating everyone who loved her as another person she needed to escape.

She wrote six pages.

Mostly about ordinary things.

She described the colour of the sea, Kamali's cooking, Ponnī's insults and a bird that stole fish from baskets with such efficiency that the fishermen had begun calling it their supervisor.

She ended by saying she missed Ilā.

Then she added a final line.

Please don't tell anyone where I am. Not even Sēnan. I know it's unfair to ask, but I trust you.

The letter never reached her.

When Analā discovered what the traveller had done, she was furious. Then she was sad.

A few days later, she began another letter.

This time she chose the carrier more carefully.

Ilā's reply came months later. She wrote about Mēni, Sēnan, the children and all the ordinary nonsense Analā had missed.

At the end, she added:

Your secret is safe. But if you ever disappear for two years again, I shall come looking for you myself.

Gradually, Analā's world became larger.

Not because she grew less cautious.

Because she grew more capable of deciding when caution was necessary.

She learned that work could be offered honestly and disagreement without malice. She grew better at distinguishing a bad bargain from a dangerous invitation, at knowing when to argue, walk away or ask someone to stand beside her.

And she learned that accepting help did not place her under a permanent debt of obedience.

Kamali could feed her.

Ponnī could teach her.

A fisherman could lend her tools.

She could be grateful without becoming theirs.

Some months she earned little.

Some months she saved enough to replace a worn tool or buy a better sleeping mat.

Eventually she moved into a room of her own near the shore.

It was small.

The roof leaked in one corner during heavy rain.

The door bolt still required a particular lift to close properly.

But the room belonged to her for as long as she paid its rent, and she had negotiated that rent herself.

On the first evening she sat on the floor and ate rice with lentils from a clay bowl.

No servants waited.

No guards stood outside.

No one had prepared a place for her at a royal table.

She was alone. Sometimes that still frightened her, but not tonight.

She looked around the room.

Her tools lay on the shelf beside folded clothes, the water jar and a little lacquered box containing her savings.

There was nothing extraordinary about any of it.

She had once lived among objects worth more than everything in the room combined.

Yet she found herself smiling.

Not because this life was better than the palace in every respect.

There were mornings when she missed soft bedding, warm water brought without asking and food that appeared before hunger became uncomfortable.

There were evenings when she missed her father so badly that she sat with a letter half-written and could not decide whether to finish it.

She did not pretend otherwise. But wanting comfort was different from surrendering the life she had chosen. She understood that much better now, and it seemed sufficient.

Seven years later, walking beside the turquoise water of Nīlathurai, Analā remembered those first years with an affection she could not have imagined feeling while she lived them.

She would have asked more questions before accepting work, hidden more money, avoided the alley and never trusted Mādhavan merely because he admired Ravana. She might even have listened more carefully to Trijata.

But she would not have gone back simply to escape the consequences.

That was something she knew.

Leaving had not been wise in every respect, and the world had often punished her foolishness rather than rewarded her courage.

But because, somewhere between the first stolen wages and the little room beside the shore, Analā had stopped treating freedom as proof that she was right.

She could be wrong, frightened, in need of help, even willing to change her mind. None of it required her to surrender the right to choose.

A warm breeze moved across the beach.

Farther ahead, a cluster of children had gathered around a tide pool. Two were arguing about a shell. A third was singing something in a voice both shrill and confident.

Analā smiled.

She recognised the tune before she recognised the words.

It was an island song.

But it was not quite the song she remembered.

The man arrived on a Tuesday afternoon, when the fishing boats had already returned and the harbour was beginning to smell of drying nets.

Analā was sitting beneath the awning of Ponnī's old workshop, repairing the lower seam of a sail. A boy had brought it that morning, folded badly and still wet with seawater. She had spread it across two wooden benches and was working her way along a tear that looked considerably worse than it was.

The stranger stood watching her for some time.

She noticed him because he did not behave like a customer.

He did not examine the sail, ask about prices or complain that somebody else's work was taking too long. He stood a little way from the awning, studying the harbour with the patient attention of a man who had learned never to appear interested in the thing that interested him.

He was perhaps sixty. His hair had gone grey unevenly, leaving dark patches around his temples. His clothes were those of a travelling teacher or minor scholar, clean but much repaired. A narrow cotton bag hung from his shoulder. His sandals had been resoled with leather of a different colour.

Nothing about him demanded attention.

That, Analā would later understand, was the first remarkable thing.

He approached when she paused to draw another length of thread through her needle.

"Your name is Analā."

She looked up.

"Who wants to know?"

"Someone who has travelled rather a long way."

"Then you should sit. Travelling makes people tired."

"I've noticed."

He did not sit.

Analā waited.

The man glanced at the sail spread before her.

"Good work."

"You haven't examined it."

"I can see enough."

"Then you're more easily satisfied than my customers."

He smiled faintly.

"Possibly."

Something in the exchange made her uneasy.

He had not asked her name.

He had stated it.

And there was something about his manner that suggested he had expected her answer before she gave it.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"A teacher."

"Of what?"

"Several things. Mostly children."

"Then you've come to the wrong workshop."

"I don't think so."

He looked at her directly.

"Your father is Vibhishana."

The needle stopped between her fingers.

For a moment the sounds of the harbour seemed to withdraw. She could still see the boy carrying fish past the awning, the sails moving above the quay, the little dog worrying a piece of rope. But they had become objects at the far end of a room.

Analā set down the needle.

"How did you find me?"

The man did not answer.

She looked towards the harbour.

Three fishing vessels were unloading beside the landing steps. Farther out, a trading boat had anchored beyond the shallow reef.

Was somebody waiting aboard?

Had her father finally sent men to bring her home?

She had imagined this moment often enough during her first years in Nīlathurai. Less frequently as time passed. By the fifth year she had begun to think that perhaps she had disappeared successfully.

Now a stranger stood before her, speaking her father's name.

"Are you alone?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Are you certain?"

He regarded her with mild interest.

"About being alone?"

"About what I'm asking."

"Quite certain."

She rose.

The sail slipped from her knees.

"Has my father sent you?"

"No."

"Then why do you know who I am?"

"Because I was looking for you."

Her mouth went dry.

She thought of Kamali's house, of the road towards Uchipuli, of the fishing boats that sometimes left before sunset.

She had money hidden beneath the floor of her room.

Not much.

Enough to leave.

She hated that she had begun calculating already.

"Am I to be taken back to Lanka?"

"No."

"You expect me to believe that?"

"Not particularly."

He said it without offence.

Then he looked towards the opposite end of the awning, where two fishermen were arguing about the ownership of a basket.

"Perhaps we should speak somewhere less public."

Analā almost laughed.

The last man who had invited her somewhere private had taught her a lesson she had not forgotten.

"No."

The stranger considered this.

"Very sensible."

He drew a low stool towards the open side of the workshop, where anyone passing could see them, and sat down.

"Here, then."

Analā remained standing.

"You haven't told me your name."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because names are sometimes more useful after introductions."

"That doesn't make sense."

"It usually does eventually."

She disliked him.

She also had the uncomfortable impression that he knew she disliked him and was perfectly content with the arrangement.

"Your father did not send me," the stranger repeated. "I have not spoken to him in many years."

"Then how do you know about me?"

"I know that you returned to Lanka after the war. That you spent some time in the palace. That you left. And that you were seen afterwards travelling alone towards the northwestern coast."

"Who told you?"

"Different people."

"Which people?"

"People who remembered a young woman asking about passage, or repairing nets, or paying for a journey she did not complete."

Analā felt her face grow hot.

"The island boat."

"Yes."

She stared at him.

"That was nearly six years ago."

"Five years and seven months."

Analā stared at him.

"You know that precisely?"

"I find that knowing when something happened is often useful when trying to discover what happened next."

She was beginning to understand why her uncle had employed him.

"How did you know about it?"

"A young woman leaving her name on one vessel and travelling on another is not the worst deception I have encountered."

"Then why did it take you so long to find me?"

"It didn't."

He folded his hands.

"I only began looking recently."

She was silent.

"A woman who knows the island methods of joining rope," he continued, "who speaks with a Lankan accent and refuses to discuss her family, is not particularly difficult to describe. Finding one is harder. But there are only so many coastal settlements, and merchants have better memories for useful craftsmen than kings have for their own proclamations."

"Craftswomen," Analā corrected automatically.

He inclined his head.

"Of course."

She disliked him a little less.

Then she remembered why he was there.

"Are you a spy?"

He looked at her for a long moment.

"I used to be."

"For whom?"

"Your uncle."

The harbour seemed to become quiet again.

Analā sat down slowly.

"My uncle?"

"Yes."

"Ravana?"

The man's face changed.

Not much.

But something passed through it that had not been present when he spoke of her father.

"He was my king," he said.

Analā stared.

There had been hundreds of men who served Ravana.

Soldiers. Officials. Engineers. Physicians. Scribes.

But the stranger had said spy.

She remembered hearing her uncle talk about intelligence reports, enemy movements and men who returned with information that could not be obtained openly. She remembered him laughing at the extraordinary things his informants sometimes believed.

She had never known their names.

"Who are you?"

The man glanced once towards the road.

Then he said, quietly, "Shuka."

The name meant nothing to her.

He seemed unsurprised.

"I worked in your uncle's intelligence service. For a long time I was the man he sent to discover what other people wished he did not know."

"Then you must have known him well."

"Better than was always comfortable."

She looked at the worn clothes, the carefully mended sandals, the unremarkable cotton bag.

"You don't look like a spy."

"That would have been a difficult profession if I did."

For the first time Analā smiled.

She did not intend to.

He noticed.

"Where have you been?" she asked.

"On the mainland."

"All this time?"

"Most of it."

"Doing what?"

"Teaching."

She looked sceptical.

He accepted this without protest.

"Mathematics. Music when anyone is willing to learn it properly. A little astronomy. Enough writing to make children dangerous to dishonest merchants."

"Where?"

"Near the Kaveri. Not far from Tiruchirappalli."

"Under your own name?"

"No."

"Then what name?"

He told her.

It was an ordinary name, the kind that belonged to a hundred men along the river.

She repeated it.

It sounded nothing like Shuka.

"Does anyone know?"

"That I was a spy?"

She nodded.

"My students know that I am a tiresome old teacher. Their parents know that I insist upon payment before the planting season. The village knows that I can repair a veena and am unnecessarily rude about inaccurate calendars."

"And the truth?"

"Some know I came from Lanka."

"That's all?"

"Usually enough."

She studied him.

"Why did you leave?"

"Because the war ended."

"That isn't an answer."

"No. But it is a beginning."

He looked towards the water.

"After the fighting, the people who had served your uncle discovered that their usefulness depended upon what they were prepared to remember. Some made their peace with the new government. Some had families to protect. Some simply wanted to live."

"And you?"

"I had been asked to remember rather more than was convenient."

"By my uncle?"

"Yes."

Analā waited.

He did not continue immediately.

A fisherman came to collect a repaired net. She handed it over, accepted his payment and watched him examine the knots.

When he had gone, Shuka said, "Your uncle knew that if he lost the war, people would tell a different story about him."

"Everyone knew that."

"Not quite."

He leaned forward.

"Most men understand that their enemies will speak badly of them. Your uncle understood something more dangerous. He understood that people would eventually forget there had ever been another way to speak."

Analā looked at him.

"Did he know he would lose?"

"No."

The answer came immediately.

"He knew he might."

For a while they spoke of the war.

Shuka did not tell the story as the songs told it.

He spoke of engineering failures, hungry soldiers, arguments over water, stores that had caught fire and men who remained at their posts because nobody had told them they were permitted to leave.

He spoke of Ravana's anger.

His pride.

His extraordinary capacity to understand a difficult problem and his equally extraordinary reluctance, at times, to accept a simple answer.

Analā listened.

Some things sounded familiar.

Others troubled her.

"He was not always right," Shuka said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

She looked at him sharply.

"He took Sita."

"Yes."

"He knew it was wrong."

"Eventually he said so."

"And he let her go."

"He opened her gate. That matters. It does not make the taking disappear."

Analā looked away.

She knew this.

She had known it for years.

Yet hearing it from a man who had stood beside her uncle unsettled her more than hearing it from his enemies.

Shuka watched the harbour.

"Your uncle was capable of great generosity," he said. "And great damage. I have seen him save a man whose name he never learned. I have also watched him defend a decision long after he understood what it would cost."

"Then why did you stay with him?"

Shuka smiled sadly.

"Because I loved him."

It was so uncomplicated an answer that Analā did not know what to say.

"And because leaving would not have made the things I had already done disappear."

For several moments neither spoke.

Then Analā asked the question she had carried for years.

"Were you with him when the children left?"

"Yes."

"On the cliff?"

"Yes."

She looked towards the sea.

"I saw him from the boat."

Shuka turned.

"I know."

Her eyes returned to his face.

"How?"

"Because I was standing beside him."

The light was changing.

The bright water beyond the harbour had begun to darken towards the east. Shadows lengthened beneath the boats drawn up onshore. Somewhere behind the workshops, a woman called her children to supper.

Analā scarcely noticed.

"Tell me," she said.

Shuka rested his hands upon his knees.

"The flying frame had just launched. We had watched the pilot take it beyond the reef. The lamps were working, though one of the controls was responding too slowly. Your uncle ordered the boats to follow the signals."

"I remember the lights."

"Three hundred children were being carried away. Some were ill. Some had no parents left to accompany them. The tide was turning. We were afraid that the final boats might not clear the coast."

"Did he know I was there?"

Shuka considered.

"He knew children from the palace households had been taken aboard. I cannot tell you whether he knew which boat carried you."

"Did he see me wave?"

The question escaped before she could stop it.

For years she had carried the memory of lifting her hand towards the cliff.

For years she had wondered whether her uncle had seen it.

Shuka looked at her gently.

"I don't know."

The answer disappointed her.

She was glad he had not invented another.

"He watched the boats for a long time," Shuka said. "Longer than the officers wanted him to. He kept asking how many had crossed."

"And when they were safe?"

"He was relieved."

"Did he smile?"

"No."

She lowered her eyes.

"He was frightened," Shuka said.

Analā looked up.

"My uncle?"

"Very frightened."

She tried to imagine it.

She remembered Ravana laughing in the palace corridors, complaining about the placement of instruments, asking whether she had eaten, correcting calculations with a patience he seldom extended to political arguments.

She remembered his hand upon her head.

She had never imagined that hand trembling.

Shuka had.

"His hand shook," he said. "He pressed it against the stone wall to steady it. He knew I had seen."

"What did he say?"

"That fear was information."

Analā frowned.

"Information?"

"Yes."

"He said that?"

"He did."

"What did he mean?"

"I asked him."

Shuka's voice had become quieter.

"He said it told him that he wanted to live."

Analā felt something move painfully inside her chest.

She looked towards the distant shoreline.

The cliff was too far away to see from Nīlathurai.

Yet for an instant she could almost see it.

The dark rock.

The launching rail.

The boats below.

A man standing above the water, one hand pressed against stone because he did not wish his friend to see it shaking.

"He wanted to live," she repeated.

"Very much."

"Then why didn't he leave?"

"I cannot tell you everything he was thinking."

"But you knew him."

"That is precisely why I know how much of another man's mind remains closed, even to those who love him."

Analā looked down at her hands.

"He could have come with us."

"Yes."

"He could have taken another boat."

"Possibly."

"And he stayed."

"Yes."

She was silent for a long time.

Then she said, "I always thought he was brave because he wasn't afraid."

Shuka shook his head.

"No. He was brave because he was."

Analā wiped her eyes with the back of her hand.

She had not realised she was crying.

Shuka said nothing.

He allowed her the silence.

It was Analā who spoke again.

"You said he knew what people would say about him."

"Yes."

"How much did he know?"

Shuka sighed.

"More than I believed possible at the time."

He told her about the painted cloth.

The ten-headed monster.

The grotesque images of her aunt and uncle.

The sleeping giant who had once commanded soldiers.

The victorious prince whose doubts would become virtues and whose victories would become proof that he had always been righteous.

He told her about the names.

How Ravana had feared that parents would give Rama's name to their sons as a blessing, while his own would become something people used to frighten children.

And about the effigy.

"They will build me tall enough to be seen across a field," Shuka recalled him saying.

He described the straw figure, the painted heads and the burning arrow.

Analā listened with growing unease.

She had seen smaller versions of such figures.

She had heard people laugh at the monstrous king.

She had heard children repeat stories that would have horrified the engineers and musicians who once served in Ravana's palace.

"How could he know?" she whispered.

"He understood people. He understood power. And he understood what happens to memory when one side is permitted to speak for everyone."

"But how could he know so precisely?"

"He did not know."

Shuka's voice was firm.

"He predicted. There is a difference. I thought him angry and perhaps a little extravagant. Some of what he imagined has come to pass. Other things may never happen. We must not turn his understanding of people into another form of magic."

Analā considered this.

Then a thought came to her.

It was so troubling that she almost refused to say it.

"Do you think he knew the children would remember him?"

Shuka looked at her.

"What do you mean?"

"He sent us away. Hundreds of us. He could have saved more soldiers, or more records, or even himself."

"He was trying to save lives."

"I know."

But she was no longer certain that the answer was complete.

"He understood how stories survive. He knew songs would change. He knew children would grow up and tell their own children what they remembered."

Shuka waited.

"Do you think that was why he saved us?"

The words hurt her as she spoke them.

For years the evacuation had been the purest memory she possessed of her uncle.

He had saved children.

Not warriors.

Not powerful men whose loyalty might secure him another victory.

Children.

And now, listening to the accuracy of his predictions, she wondered whether even that act had contained a calculation.

Had they been lives to him?

Or witnesses?

Shuka looked towards the boats in the harbour.

"I cannot answer that for him."

Analā felt disappointed.

She had begun to understand that Shuka would not give comforting answers simply because they were wanted.

He continued.

"I saw him refusing to leave while those boats were still in danger. I saw his relief when the first signal came from the island. I saw him insist that every child's name be recorded, even when the city was burning."

She remembered Sēnan's lists.

The names.

The descriptions of children whose names nobody had known.

"Perhaps he understood that some of you would remember him," Shuka said. "He was certainly capable of understanding such a thing."

"Then it was a calculation."

"Perhaps it was also a calculation."

She looked at him.

He smiled faintly.

"Analā, have you never done a kind thing knowing it might also do some good for you?"

She thought about the question.

Of course she had.

She had helped fishermen who later offered her work. She had lent tools to neighbours whose friendship made her life easier. She had written to Ilā because she wanted her friend to know she was alive, but also because she could no longer bear being forgotten.

"Does that make the kindness false?" Shuka asked.

"No."

"Then perhaps your uncle deserves the same complication."

She looked down.

"Do you believe he loved us?"

"Yes."

"All those children?"

"I believe he loved the idea that they might live. That they would have mornings he would never see. That they might grow into people who would argue about things other than war."

Shuka paused.

"He was a man who could calculate the movement of an army and still become furious when somebody forgot to feed a child."

Analā smiled through her tears.

"That sounds like him."

"It does."

She thought for a moment.

"Perhaps he wanted us to remember."

"Perhaps."

"And perhaps he wanted us to live."

"Yes."

"Both."

Shuka nodded.

"Both."

For the first time, the possibility did not diminish the memory.

It made her uncle seem closer.

Not a perfect man performing a perfect sacrifice.

A man who had seen several possible futures, feared some of them, hoped for others, and made a choice while the walls around him were collapsing.

She could understand that.

Better now than she could have at fifteen.

Shuka reached into his cotton bag.

He removed a small piece of metal wrapped in an old cloth.

Analā watched him unfold it.

The plate was no larger than his palm. Fine lines had been scratched across its surface, some close together, others separated by wider spaces.

It looked like the sort of thing a careless merchant might discard.

"What is it?"

"Your uncle's."

She took a breath.

"His?"

"From his veena. The markings were used to set the strings correctly."

She leaned closer.

She remembered the instrument.

The dark wood.

The polished gourd.

The way her uncle sometimes stopped speaking halfway through a conversation because a musical idea had entered his mind and demanded more attention than the person sitting opposite him.

"Can I touch it?"

Shuka handed it to her.

The metal was warm from his hand.

Analā traced one of the marks with her thumb.

"He gave this to you?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because he feared the things that mattered about him would be lost."

She looked at the plate.

It was such a small object.

No royal seal.

No proclamation.

No inscription declaring who had owned it.

Only marks left by a musician who wanted his instrument to sound correctly.

Shuka told her what her uncle had said.

That he did not want his innocence preserved.

That he wanted people to remember he had been more than the worst thing he had done.

Analā closed her eyes.

For a moment she could hear the veena.

Not a particular melody.

Only the soft vibration of a string being tuned, followed by the silence in which her uncle decided whether the note was right.

She returned the plate.

Shuka wrapped it carefully.

"Have you kept it all these years?"

"Yes."

"Why not give it to someone who could show the world?"

"Who?"

She opened her mouth.

Then closed it.

He waited.

"The court?" he asked. "A temple? A victorious prince who could announce that the monster had once played music?"

Analā looked away.

"It would become another object whose meaning belonged to whoever possessed it," Shuka said.

"Then what will you do with it?"

"For now, keep it."

"Until when?"

"I don't know."

He replaced the plate in his bag.

There was no solemnity in the movement.

Only the care of a man putting away something that could not easily be replaced.

"Why did you come looking for me?" Analā asked.

She had almost forgotten that the question remained unanswered.

Shuka looked towards the harbour.

"Because I have heard stories."

"About my uncle?"

"Yes."

"Everyone tells stories about him."

"These were different."

He described a song he had heard near the Kaveri.

It spoke of children escaping a burning city.

Of boats crossing black water.

Of a king who remained upon a cliff while the youngest were carried away.

He had heard another version farther south.

And a third from a trader who had visited the coast.

The details were not identical.

Some were already changing.

In one version the king had carried the boats himself.

In another he had stood upon the sea and commanded the waves.

Shuka smiled without pleasure.

"Your uncle would have been irritated by both."

Analā laughed.

"He would have asked who had built the boats."

"And whether the sea had submitted a report."

She laughed again.

Then she became quiet.

"Where did the songs come from?"

"That is what I wanted to ask you."

"Why me?"

"Because one of them mentions the children of the island. Not simply children who escaped the war, but children who lived together afterwards. It speaks of a woman who fed them, an old man who kept their names, and a girl who taught the younger ones to sing."

Analā stared at him.

"Ilā."

"Who?"

"Ilā. My friend."

Shuka waited.

"She began making songs on the island," Analā said. "At first they were little things. Stories for the children. Some of them were about my uncle."

"Did she know him?"

"No."

"Then how did she learn?"

"From me. From the other children. From people who remembered."

Shuka sat back.

For some time he said nothing.

Analā watched his expression.

She could not tell whether he was disappointed.

"Is that wrong?" she asked.

"No."

"Some of the stories aren't entirely true."

"I know."

"I argued with her about it."

"Did you?"

"Often."

He smiled.

"Good."

"But she said a story had to be remembered if it was to survive."

Shuka looked at the sea.

"She isn't wrong."

"Neither was I."

"No."

Analā frowned.

"Then which of us was right?"

Shuka regarded her with something close to amusement.

"Your uncle spent a considerable portion of his life discovering that two people could be right about different parts of the same problem."

"And then?"

"Usually he argued with both."

Analā laughed.

Shuka continued.

"Songs travel farther than records. They survive where inscriptions break and witnesses die. But they change in the travelling."

"Then what should Ilā do?"

"I wouldn't presume to instruct a girl who has managed to carry a story across the sea without knowing she was doing it."

He looked at her.

"But perhaps she should sometimes be reminded that the king who saved the children was also the man whose decisions helped place them in danger."

Analā lowered her eyes.

"She knows."

"Then she may already understand more than many older storytellers."

The afternoon was nearly gone.

Shuka rose.

Analā stood with him.

"You're leaving?"

"Yes."

"So soon?"

"I came to find you. I have found you."

"And now?"

"I have learned what I needed to learn."

"About the children?"

"About where some of them went. About what they remember. About the stories they have begun to tell."

He adjusted the strap of his bag.

"That is enough for one journey."

Analā walked with him towards the edge of the harbour road.

"Will you tell my father where I am?"

"No."

"How do I know?"

"You don't."

She stopped.

He turned towards her.

"If I meant to return you to Lanka, Analā, I would not have arrived alone, told you my name and spent an afternoon discussing your uncle's music."

She considered that.

It was a reasonable argument.

She had learned not to mistake reasonable arguments for promises.

"Then promise me."

Shuka looked at her.

"I promise."

She nodded.

For some reason the simplicity of it satisfied her more than his explanation.

"Will you return?"

"I don't expect to."

"Why?"

"Because I have no reason to."

There was no cruelty in the answer.

He seemed to find it an entirely ordinary fact.

Analā looked towards the road.

"Then I should thank you."

"For what?"

"For telling me about the cliff."

Shuka inclined his head.

"He would have wanted you to know he was frightened," she said.

"I don't know that."

She smiled.

"You really don't give people anything they haven't earned, do you?"

"Your uncle accused me of the opposite."

They stood together for a moment.

Then Analā asked, "Did he give you any final instruction?"

Shuka's expression changed.

He seemed to look past her, towards a room she would never see, where smoke had entered beneath a door and a water clock had continued measuring the minutes.

"He told me to find those who remembered," he said. "And to carry what I could without making it another weapon."

"Have you done that?"

"Some of it."

"And the rest?"

Shuka looked at the harbour, at the boats returning beneath the evening sky.

"I asked him what I should do if they sang his story wrongly."

Analā waited.

"He told me to sing longer."

She repeated the words silently.

Sing longer.

She thought of Ilā sitting among the children, arguing about verses.

She thought of the stories travelling through markets and harbours, changing as they went.

She thought of her uncle's name, spoken as a curse in one village and with affection in another.

"Perhaps someone already is," she said.

Shuka looked at her.

"Perhaps."

He smiled then.

It was the first smile she had seen that afternoon which seemed to belong entirely to the man rather than to the profession he had once practised.

He turned towards the road.

Analā watched him go.

He did not look back.

There was no escort waiting beyond the houses. No royal vessel moving towards the harbour. No hidden soldiers emerging from the market.

Only an ageing teacher carrying a cotton bag, walking towards the evening road.

She wondered whether anyone would ever know who he had been.

She wondered whether the children he taught near the Kaveri would remember him as anything more than a difficult old man who corrected their sums and insisted they tune their instruments properly.

Perhaps that was enough.

She went back to the workshop.

The sail still lay across the benches.

Her needle remained where she had left it.

A fisherman was waiting to ask whether his repair would be ready before morning.

Analā looked at the torn canvas, the unfinished stitching and the man who had been patient enough to wait.

"Tomorrow," she told him.

He began to protest.

She raised one hand.

"Tomorrow."

He looked at her face and thought better of arguing.

That evening, after she had eaten, Analā took out the latest letter from Ilā.

She unfolded it beneath the lamp.

There were stories of the island, news of Mēni, complaints about Sēnan's increasingly elaborate records, and descriptions of children who had grown tall enough to begin pretending they no longer needed anybody.

And there were songs.

Always songs.

Analā had read them before.

Tonight she read them differently.

Some lines were beautiful.

Some were foolish.

Some described things her uncle had never done.

She could almost hear him objecting.

She smiled.

Then she reached for a fresh sheet of paper.

For a long time she sat without writing.

At last she dipped her pen.

Ilā, she began.

Today I met someone who knew my uncle.

She stopped.

Then she continued.

There is something I think you should know about the songs.

The tide had begun to turn.

Analā could tell by the small changes along the shore: the wet sand widening beneath the fishing boats, the dark rocks emerging beyond the eastern curve, the children moving their collection of shells farther down the beach because the water had stopped reaching them.

She had been walking slowly enough that the sun was now considerably higher than when she left home.

Ahead, Nīlathurai's harbour was busy.

Men carried baskets between boats and stalls. A pair of oxen stood beside a cart loaded with salt, their tails moving lazily against the flies. Someone was repairing a wooden wheel beneath a palm-leaf shelter. Beyond the landing steps, a merchant vessel waited for its cargo to be weighed.

Seven years.

That was how long she had lived away from Lanka.

It seemed an extraordinary length of time when she remembered the frightened girl hiding behind grain sacks on a departing boat.

It seemed very little when she considered how much of that girl's life she still carried.

The first years had been difficult.

The later ones had not been free of difficulty, but somewhere along the way she had stopped measuring her life by what she had escaped.

There were things she looked forward to now.

A new sail being delivered for repair. The first fishing boats returning after the monsoon. Kamali's increasingly unreasonable attempts to improve her cooking. The letters from Ilā.

Especially the letters.

Analā smiled.

The first reply had taken months to reach her.

Now, seven years after she had left, she had enough letters to fill a wooden box beneath her sleeping mat. Some were written neatly. Others had been composed in such haste that the words leaned into one another as though trying to reach the end of the page before the writer changed her mind.

There were stories of the island.

Of Sēnan's worsening habit of counting everything.

Of Mēni's insistence that children who had survived a war could nevertheless be expected to wash their own plates.

Of marriages, births, departures and disagreements.

And increasingly, there were songs.

Analā had watched those songs change over the years.

At first she had tried to correct every detail.

Later she had learned that correcting a song was considerably more difficult than correcting a net.

A net, once repaired, generally stayed repaired.

A song could be altered by anyone who remembered it badly.

The thought made her laugh.

She had learned that lesson particularly well as the songs began to travel.

The first new song arrived in a letter from Ilā when Analā was twenty.

It was longer than anything Ilā had written before.

The verses had been copied carefully onto several sheets, with small marks above certain words to indicate where the melody rose or fell.

Ilā explained that a travelling merchant had heard children singing one of her older songs and had asked whether there were more.

He had carried the tune to another settlement.

There, somebody had added a verse.

Another traveller had brought it back.

Ilā had been delighted.

Then she had discovered that the returning version was not quite the one she had written.

In her letter she complained:

Someone has made your uncle carry all the boats across the sea himself. I don't know how he managed it while standing on the cliff.

Analā had laughed aloud.

The following page contained a second complaint.

They have given him ten arms. I told them he had two. A woman said that was not enough arms for such a great king.

Analā laughed again.

But as she continued reading, she discovered that the songs were changing in ways Ilā had not anticipated.

They were no longer principally about the evacuation.

There were songs about Ravana the musician.

Ravana the scholar.

Ravana the king who had mastered the movements of the heavens.

Ravana the devotee who sang to Shiva.

Some verses were beautiful.

Others were extraordinary.

One described the young Ravana studying beneath a mountain until the gods themselves came to hear him recite.

Another claimed that he understood every language spoken by men, birds and animals.

A third told of Shiva granting him strength greater than that of ordinary kings.

Analā sat with the pages spread across her knees.

She remembered her uncle teaching her how to identify the brightest stars.

He had been patient until she became distracted.

Then he had complained that the stars were doing their part and she ought to do hers.

She remembered his veena.

She remembered the way he could make a room fall silent by touching a string.

She remembered him arguing with a physician about the proper preparation of medicine.

But she did not remember gods descending to grant him powers.

Nor did she remember him understanding the language of birds.

The last claim would have amused him particularly.

He had once spent half an afternoon trying to discover why a parrot in the palace gardens had learned to imitate the chief accountant.

The bird had proved more difficult to question than the accountant.

Analā smiled at the memory.

Then she returned to the songs.

One verse described Ravana's devotion to Shiva.

He climbed a mountain, the song said, carrying his veena upon his back. He played until the rocks themselves trembled. Shiva, delighted by the music, appeared before him and offered a gift.

Ravana asked for neither wealth nor land.

He asked for knowledge.

Analā read the verse twice.

She had never heard such a story from her uncle.

But she could imagine him enjoying it.

Especially the part in which a king was offered wealth and chose something more interesting.

She could also imagine him complaining that the mountain had been made unnecessarily difficult to climb.

She wrote to Ilā.

He did love Shiva. And he did play beautifully. But I never saw a mountain tremble because of his music.

Ilā replied several months later.

Perhaps you were standing on the wrong mountain.

Analā laughed so hard that Kamali came from the next room to ask what had happened.

Over the following years, the stories grew.

Travellers brought them to Nīlathurai without knowing that a woman repairing sails beside the harbour had once sat upon the lap of the man they were singing about.

Analā heard them in markets.

Beside cooking fires.

From sailors who had learned the verses in other ports.

Some praised Ravana's learning.

They said he had mastered the Vedas and all the sciences known to his age.

They called him a great sage who had become a king, rather than a king who had occasionally found time for learning.

They told of his skill in music, his command of the veena, his knowledge of healing and the movement of stars.

They described Lanka as a city of gold.

Analā had lived in that city.

She knew its roofs had leaked.

Its streets had flooded during heavy rain.

Its markets contained dishonest merchants, argumentative customers and men who drank too much.

There had been beautiful buildings.

There had also been broken drains.

She wondered whether the singers knew that.

Probably not.

Gold was easier to remember than drainage.

The most extravagant songs concerned Shiva.

In one, Ravana had prayed so faithfully that Shiva made him invincible.

In another, he had lifted an entire mountain to prove his devotion.

In a third, he offered his own heads to Shiva, one after another, until the god restored them and blessed him.

Analā listened to that song beside a harbour fire, surrounded by fishermen who sang the refrain with great enthusiasm.

She recognised what had happened.

The ten disciplines her uncle had mastered had become ten heads.

Now those heads had become offerings.

The offerings had become proof of devotion.

And devotion had become the reason the gods granted him extraordinary powers.

A story had grown upon another story until nobody could see where the first one ended.

When the singer finished, the fishermen applauded.

Analā did not.

A young man noticed.

"Don't you like the song?"

"It's very beautiful."

"Then why aren't you smiling?"

"I'm wondering how he managed to play the veena afterwards."

The young man looked confused.

"After what?"

"After giving away all his heads."

For a moment he stared at her.

Then the other fishermen began laughing.

The singer, who had consumed considerably more palm wine than was advisable before attempting theology, declared that divine gifts were not required to obey ordinary rules.

Analā conceded that this was a convenient arrangement.

She did not tell him who she was.

There had been a time when she would have been furious.

Now she was mostly curious.

What began as admiration could become marvel.

What became marvel could become miracle.

And a miracle, once accepted, no longer needed an explanation.

Not all the songs were kind.

Some travellers still brought the older accounts from the victorious kingdoms.

Ravana the monster.

Ravana the thief.

Ravana the creature of ten heads who had dared to challenge a god.

Some made the war a simple contest between virtue and evil.

Others made the people of Lanka monstrous so that their defeat required no further thought.

Analā had learned to listen without answering every insult.

But there was another kind of song that troubled her more than she expected.

It was sung by people who admired Ravana.

They called him a great king.

A scholar.

A musician.

A devotee whose faith had earned Shiva's blessing.

Then they spoke of Sita.

And their voices became certain.

Ravana had been so honourable, they said, that he had never touched her.

He had kept her in a beautiful garden.

He had offered her comfort and protection.

He had waited patiently for her affection.

Some claimed he had never intended her harm.

Others went farther.

They said the taking itself had been misunderstood.

That he had acted only to defend his sister's honour.

That he had never committed any wrong at all.

Analā first heard one such song from a travelling performer who had come to Nīlathurai during a festival.

The gathering was cheerful. Children sat near the front. Women passed around bowls of roasted grain. The performer sang well, and his listeners followed the refrain eagerly.

When he reached the verses about Sita, he lifted his voice.

He sang of a noble king who possessed extraordinary power yet refused to touch a woman who did not love him.

The audience murmured its approval.

A man near Analā said, "That proves he was good."

Analā turned.

"Does it?"

The man looked surprised.

"Of course."

"He took her away from her home."

"But he did not touch her."

"She could not leave."

"He treated her with honour."

Analā looked towards the singer.

She remembered the garden in Lanka.

The locked gate.

The women who had tended Sita.

She remembered her uncle's kindness.

And she remembered that his kindness had not opened the gate until the end.

"Perhaps," she said quietly, "a man can be better than people say without being innocent of what he did."

The man frowned.

"Are you saying the song is wrong?"

"I'm saying it has left something out."

He considered her.

Then he shrugged.

"It is a song."

Analā almost smiled.

She had heard that answer before.

Not in those words.

But close enough.

She thought of Ilā.

Of their arguments beside the water jars.

Of the smaller children forgetting the verses unless Ilā made them memorable.

And she understood something she had resisted for years.

A song could preserve a memory.

It could also protect people from the discomfort of remembering too much.

The songs that made Ravana a monster were doing that.

So were some of the songs that made him entirely good.

She wrote to Ilā again.

This time the letter was longer than usual.

She described the songs she had heard.

The mountain.

The ten heads offered to Shiva.

The knowledge granted by the gods.

The great scholar who could command the heavens.

The king whose golden city apparently contained no ordinary people.

And the noble Ravana who had done nothing wrong in taking Sita.

She wrote that some of the verses were beautiful.

She also wrote that she was frightened by how easily love became certainty.

Ilā's reply arrived near the end of the dry season.

I didn't write all those things, she began.

Analā smiled.

She knew that already.

Ilā explained that the songs had travelled beyond the island. People had changed them. Some had added verses from older stories. Others had taken familiar tales about gods and attached Ravana's name to them.

There were even singers who had never heard of the island and believed their versions to be ancient.

I cannot follow every song, Ilā wrote.

And I am beginning to think I should not try.

Analā read on.

You want people to remember your uncle as he was. I understand that. But I never knew him as you did. I knew the man who saved us because you told me about him. Other people know the scholar, the musician, the devotee, the king. Perhaps some of those stories are wrong. Perhaps some contain things neither of us knows.

Then came a line that made Analā stop.

I don't want to make him perfect. I only don't want him forgotten.

Analā sat with the letter for a long time.

She thought of Shuka.

Of the small metal plate he had carried.

Of the king who had feared that future generations would remember only the worst thing he had done.

She thought of Ilā, who had never met Ravana but had spent years refusing to let the children forget the man who sent them across the sea.

And she began to recognise the difficulty.

Ilā could not control the songs.

Neither could she.

There would be people who loved Ravana for reasons Analā did not understand.

People who hated him for reasons she did.

People who invented miracles to honour him.

People who invented monstrosities to condemn him.

Perhaps some would worship him.

Perhaps others would burn his image.

She could not prevent either.

And she could not make the world remember the man she remembered.

There had only ever been one such man.

He had lived.

He had made choices.

He had loved people.

He had harmed people.

And he was gone.

Everything afterwards belonged, at least partly, to those who remained.

In the years that followed, the letters between the two women became less concerned with correcting songs.

There were still arguments.

Ilā continued to enjoy making a verse more beautiful than Analā considered strictly necessary.

Analā continued to object whenever poetry required her uncle to perform an act that would have been physically impossible.

But the arguments became affectionate.

Sometimes Ilā sent a new song and asked whether Analā could bear it.

Sometimes Analā returned a corrected version with so many words crossed out that Ilā complained she had been sent a fishing net rather than a poem.

And sometimes they wrote about nothing important at all.

A wedding.

A storm.

A boat arriving late.

Mēni's cooking.

Kamali's increasingly firm opinion that Analā should learn to prepare something besides rice without burning the bottom of the pot.

They had grown older without growing together.

At fifteen, Analā had known Ilā's every habit.

The way she talked when excited.

The way she became angry when someone interrupted a song.

The way she pretended not to mind when Analā corrected her.

Now their lives had acquired people the other had never met.

Ilā had friends Analā knew only by name.

Analā had neighbours whose faces Ilā could only imagine.

They wrote often enough to remain close.

Not often enough to prevent the years from changing them.

Sometimes Analā wondered whether they would recognise one another immediately.

Then Ilā would write something so unmistakably foolish that the question disappeared.

Once she wrote:

I have decided that if I ever marry, the man must be capable of singing in tune.

Analā replied:

Then I fear you will remain unmarried.

Ilā answered:

I have already made peace with that possibility.

Analā laughed.

She carried the letter in her sash for several days, taking it out whenever work became tiresome.

The invitation came at the end of the seventh year.

It arrived with a trading vessel that had called at Sēnan's island before crossing towards the mainland.

Analā recognised Ilā's handwriting before she broke the seal.

The letter was shorter than usual.

There was news of the island.

A little about Mēni.

A complaint about the children.

Then, almost casually:

I shall be travelling to the mainland after the next full moon. There is a merchant who will bring me as far as Nīlathurai.

Analā read the sentence again.

Ilā was coming.

For seven years they had lived in one another's letters.

Now her friend intended to arrive upon an ordinary trading boat, carrying a bundle and expecting someone to meet her.

Analā looked around her room.

It suddenly seemed smaller than it had that morning.

She imagined Ilā standing inside it.

Commenting upon the roof.

Inspecting her tools.

Opening the water jar without asking.

Finding fault with her cooking.

She smiled.

Then she continued reading.

I know you have built a life there. I am not coming to tell you what to do with it.

The next line was written more carefully.

But I miss you. And I would like to see the place that has kept you from us for so long.

Analā folded the letter.

Then unfolded it.

Then read the last line again.

Also, Mēni says you are to eat properly before I arrive, because you have always become foolish when hungry.

Analā laughed.

She sat upon the floor with the letter in her hands and felt something she had not allowed herself to feel for a very long time.

Anticipation.

Not the anxious anticipation of a journey whose dangers she could not foresee.

Not the fear of an unexpected knock upon her door.

Something simpler.

Someone she loved was coming.

And she wanted her to come.

A shout from the harbour brought Analā back to the morning.

The tide had fallen farther.

She stood where the beach met the landing road, with the repaired sailcloth still over her shoulder and the wooden needle tucked into her sash.

Around her, Nīlathurai continued its ordinary business.

A cart creaked past carrying sacks of salt. Two fishermen argued about whether a net had been damaged by a rock or by somebody's incompetence. A little girl ran between them carrying a coconut larger than her head.

Analā looked towards the landing steps.

A trading vessel was approaching.

Its sail had been lowered. Two men worked the oars while another stood in the bow with a pole, testing the depth.

The harbour clerk came down from his office carrying a tablet.

He looked annoyed before anything had happened.

Analā smiled.

The boat drew alongside.

A rope came ashore.

Passengers began climbing down.

First an elderly man carrying two baskets.

Then a woman with a sleeping child.

Then a young merchant who argued about the weight of his luggage before either foot had reached the quay.

Analā watched the deck.

For a moment she could not see anyone else.

Then a figure appeared behind the raised cargo.

A young woman carrying a cloth bundle.

Her hair was longer than Analā remembered.

She had grown taller.

Her face had changed in the quiet, unmistakable way that childhood gives way to adulthood. The girl Analā remembered was still there, somewhere behind the woman's eyes, but the years had given her features a definition they had not possessed before.

She stood beside the boatman, looking towards the shore.

Analā recognised her before the woman saw her.

Ilā.

For a moment Analā did not move.

There was something almost frightening about seeing a person step out of memory and become real again.

She had known Ilā as a girl running barefoot between the houses on Sēnan's island.

A girl sitting beside a water jar, insisting that a verse required a better ending.

A girl who had once argued that stories needed to be beautiful enough for children to remember.

Now she stood on a boat in Nīlathurai, older and sun-browned, with a bundle in her arms and an expression of considerable impatience as she waited for the merchant ahead of her to finish complaining.

Analā laughed.

Ilā looked up.

Their eyes met.

For an instant neither moved.

Then Ilā shouted her name.

"Analā!"

Several people turned.

Analā lifted her hand.

Ilā began climbing down before the boatman had properly secured the plank.

"Wait!" he shouted.

"I've waited seven years!"

"That doesn't make the plank stronger!"

She came down anyway.

Analā dropped the sailcloth.

Ilā dropped her bundle.

They met halfway along the quay.

For a moment there were no words.

Ilā held her so tightly that Analā could scarcely breathe.

Analā held her back.

She could feel Ilā laughing against her shoulder.

Then she felt something wet upon her neck.

"Are you crying?" Analā whispered.

"No."

"You are."

"Then perhaps you're standing in the rain."

"There isn't any rain."

"Don't begin arguing already."

Analā laughed.

Ilā drew back and looked at her.

For a long moment they simply examined one another.

"You've changed," Ilā said.

"So have you."

"You're taller."

"So are you."

"Your hair is a disaster."

"Yours is worse."

Ilā looked delighted.

"Good. I was afraid you had become respectable."

Analā laughed again.

Behind them the boatman called out that somebody needed to remove the abandoned bundle from the landing steps.

Ilā turned.

"That would be mine."

"I assumed so."

"Will you carry it?"

"No."

"Seven years, and this is how you welcome me?"

"You can carry it yourself."

Ilā considered her.

Then she smiled.

"There she is."

Analā picked up the bundle.

Ilā looked pleased.

"I knew you'd do it."

"Only because you're blocking the harbour."

"Of course."

They moved away from the landing steps.

Analā retrieved her sailcloth, and Ilā examined the heavy stitching along its edge.

"You still do this?"

"Yes."

"Every day?"

"Most days."

"Doesn't it become boring?"

"Does singing the same song become boring?"

"Not if people keep getting the words wrong."

Analā laughed.

They walked towards the beach.

Ilā looked around with open curiosity.

At the boats.

The houses.

The palms leaning inland.

The pale sand curving towards the eastern headland.

Then she saw the sea.

She stopped.

For a long moment she said nothing.

Analā watched her.

The water was particularly beautiful that morning. The shallows shone green beneath the sunlight. Beyond them the deeper channel was a rich blue, and farther out, where the reef gave way to open water, the horizon was so clear that sea and sky seemed to meet without a line between them.

Ilā lowered her bundle.

"Oh," she said.

Analā smiled.

"Yes."

Ilā looked towards her.

"You've been living here?"

"For seven years."

"And you never told me it was this beautiful?"

"I wrote about it."

"You said the water was blue."

"It is."

"You failed to mention the rest of it."

"I thought you knew what a beach looked like."

Ilā stared at the water.

"This is not a beach. This is an argument for never doing anything useful again."

Analā laughed.

"Then you'll enjoy your visit."

Ilā looked back towards the houses.

"Which one is yours?"

"I'll show you."

"Does it have a roof?"

"Usually."

"A bed?"

"Yes."

"Food?"

"Sometimes."

Ilā regarded her gravely.

"Mēni was right to send me."

Analā shook her head.

They began walking again.

Along the shore, fishermen called greetings to Analā. A woman selling fruit waved. A boy carrying a net stopped to ask whether she could repair it before the following afternoon.

Analā told him to bring it tomorrow.

Ilā watched all this with interest.

"They know you."

"I live here."

"No. I mean they know you."

Analā looked at her.

Ilā gestured towards the boy, the fruit seller, the fishermen.

"They speak to you as though you've always belonged here."

Analā considered the observation.

She had not thought about belonging for some time.

Perhaps that was what belonging felt like.

Not arriving somewhere and being welcomed.

But waking one morning and discovering that people had begun expecting you to be there.

She looked towards the little houses above the shore.

"Perhaps I do," she said.

Ilā nodded.

She did not seem disappointed.

That pleased Analā more than she expected.

They walked in silence for a while.

Then Ilā began telling her about the journey.

The merchant who had insisted that the wind would change.

The sailor who had insisted it would not.

The argument that followed.

The fish that had fallen into somebody's bedding.

The woman who had spent an entire morning complaining about the smell of the sea while travelling upon it.

Analā listened.

It was wonderful.

Not because the stories were extraordinary.

Because they were Ilā's.

For seven years they had arrived folded into letters.

Now they came with gestures, laughter, interruptions and expressions that no written page could reproduce.

Analā had forgotten how much space Ilā occupied when she spoke.

She had also forgotten how frequently she changed subjects.

Within a few minutes they had discussed the voyage, Kamali's cooking, the age of Sēnan's youngest grandson, three weddings, a broken fishing boat and whether Ravana would have enjoyed hearing the songs about his ten heads.

"He would have hated the mathematics," Analā said.

"Why?"

"Ten heads require ten necks. Imagine the tailoring."

Ilā laughed.

"And the veena?"

"He would have asked which pair of hands was meant to play it."

Ilā's laughter grew louder.

A fisherman turned to look at them.

Analā ignored him.

For a moment the years seemed to fold upon themselves.

They were girls again, walking with water jars along Sēnan's island paths, arguing about songs and laughing when the argument became too foolish to continue.

Then Ilā reached for Analā's hand.

The gesture was so familiar that Analā did not notice it at first.

They walked together towards the eastern curve of the beach.

After a while Ilā asked about Kamali.

Analā told her.

About the fever.

About the little house.

About the painted image of Rama between the lamps.

Ilā listened more quietly than usual.

"She sounds kind," she said.

"She is."

"And Ponnī?"

"Terrifying."

"Good. Someone had to teach you manners."

"Ponnī says the same thing."

Ilā squeezed her hand.

Analā looked towards the water.

For seven years she had imagined this reunion.

She had imagined telling Ilā everything.

The difficult years.

The frightened nights.

The work.

The people who had helped her.

The things she had learned.

Now that Ilā was beside her, there seemed no urgency.

They had time.

They could talk tomorrow.

And the day after.

Or sit together without speaking.

The thought was unexpectedly peaceful.

At the eastern end of the beach the sand rose gently towards the houses. Analā's home stood beyond the palms, a modest building with a low roof and a small courtyard where she dried repaired sailcloth.

She could see its doorway from where they walked.

Ilā saw it too.

"Is that yours?"

"Yes."

She examined it.

"It's small."

"It is."

"The roof looks crooked."

"It is."

"Will it survive the monsoon?"

"It has survived several."

"Perhaps it has become stubborn from living with you."

Analā laughed.

Then they stopped near the water.

Ilā turned towards her.

For the first time since arriving, she seemed uncertain what to say.

Analā waited.

Ilā looked towards the sea.

"You know," she began, "Mēni still talks about you."

"I know."

"And Sēnan."

"I know."

"The children ask about you."

"Some of them aren't children anymore."

"That doesn't stop them asking."

Analā smiled.

Ilā continued.

"Things have changed on the island."

"Of course."

"Some people have gone. Others have come. The houses are different. Sēnan complains that nobody listens to him anymore."

"He complained about that when everybody listened to him."

Ilā laughed.

Then she grew quiet again.

"There are still people who remember you."

Analā looked at her.

"I remember them too."

Ilā nodded.

For a moment she seemed to be studying the water, searching for something among the small waves breaking over the sand.

Then she said, "I didn't come to bring you back."

"I know."

"I promised myself I wouldn't ask."

Analā said nothing.

Ilā looked at her.

"But I'm going to ask anyway."

Analā smiled faintly.

"That sounds like you."

Ilā took both her hands.

For a moment neither spoke.

Behind them, Nīlathurai continued its ordinary morning.

Children shouted along the beach.

A fisherman called for a missing basket.

Somewhere beyond the houses a woman began singing while she worked.

The sea moved gently over the sand.

Ilā looked into her friend's face.

"Are you ready to come home to the island?"

Analā smiled.
