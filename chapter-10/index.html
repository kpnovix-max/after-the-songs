After the Songs

Chapter Ten

The Other Song

The sculptor had given Ravana the wrong hand.

It was the apprentice who noticed. He had spent the morning carrying water for the masons, sorting broken fragments of sandstone by size and trying to remain useful without inviting another lecture about how apprentices were forever standing in the light. He was twelve and had already learned that men with chisels valued the space around their elbows more highly than most forms of human life.

Now he stood beneath the unfinished wall of the Kailasanatha temple in Kanchipuram, looking at the stone king struggling beneath a mountain.

“You have made his left hand larger,” he said.

The master continued working.

“His right hand is farther away.”

“His right hand is closer.”

The chisel stopped.

The boy prepared to be sent for water.

Instead the sculptor stepped down, walked to the opposite side of the panel and looked at it from the place where worshippers would stand. The left hand was larger. Ravana appeared to be lifting the mountain with one arm while the other searched for something in the dark.

“Bring the smaller chisel,” the master said.

The boy brought it without celebrating.

Above the stone king, Shiva sat with an ease that made the mountain's enormous weight seem almost an inconvenience. The goddess beside him had turned slightly, surprised by the disturbance beneath their home. Around them the mountain rose in tiers of carved creatures, trees and attendants. Below, Ravana strained upward, powerful enough to make the rock appear to tremble, and not powerful enough to move it.

The boy had heard the story many times. The king of Lanka, swollen with his own strength, had tried to lift Shiva's mountain. Shiva had pressed it down with a toe. Trapped and humbled, Ravana had sung in devotion until the god granted him mercy.

In some tellings, he sang for years. In others, for centuries. One workman insisted the king had fashioned the instrument from his own body, a detail the master forbade anyone from demonstrating during lunch.

The apprentice watched the chisel repair the hand.

“Why have you made him handsome?” he asked.

“I have made him a king.”

“People say he was a demon.”

“People say many things while I am trying to work.”

The boy waited. He had discovered that silence sometimes produced an answer where persistence produced a blow.

The master brushed sand from the stone. Ravana's face was turned towards the mountain, his teeth set, his shoulders carrying the impossible effort of a man who had not yet understood what opposed him.

“He was arrogant,” the sculptor said. “That is why the mountain is upon him.”

“But he was devoted to Shiva.”

“That is why the story does not end beneath the mountain.”

“Was he wicked, then, or was he good?”

The master put down his chisel. From the courtyard came the scrape of carts and the calls of labourers bringing stone. A priest was arguing with the supervisor over a shrine wall. Somewhere in the city, the morning market had reached the hour when sellers began to be personally offended by the prices customers proposed.

The sculptor looked at the boy.

“If a man is foolish on Monday and generous on Tuesday, which face shall I give him on Wednesday?”

“The generous one,” the boy said, after some thought.

“His wife may remember Monday.”

The boy laughed.

“So you would give him both faces?”

“I have only the one piece of stone.”

He returned to his work.

The apprentice watched dust gather upon Ravana's forehead. The master had left a small crease beside the king's mouth. It could have been pain. It could have been the beginning of a song.

“Shouldn't his face show that he has been defeated?” the boy asked.

“It does.”

“Shouldn't it show that he deserves it?”

The sculptor examined the unfinished head.

“His mistake is already in the stone,” he said. “Must I put it in his face as well?”

A workman called for the apprentice. Someone had overturned the water jar.

The boy ran.

The master worked until evening, when the sandstone turned the colour of old honey and the temple's unfinished towers laid long shadows across the court. Before leaving, he brushed a final line of dust from the figure below the mountain.

The face remained neither gentle nor monstrous. It was the face of a man who had done something foolish and discovered that great strength could not rescue him from the consequence.

Above him, Shiva's hand was raised in blessing.

Years later, the apprentice would remember the master saying that a chisel could remove what was unnecessary but could not put back the stone once it had fallen. He remembered this whenever a patron asked him to make a figure more beautiful, more savage or more certain than the stone permitted.

He did not remember his master's name as clearly as he remembered the line beside Ravana's mouth.

And the line remained.

In Jodhpur, the wedding procession was late, and the bride's grandmother considered this an affront to every person who had washed a cooking vessel since sunrise.

The bride was not concerned. She stood in an upstairs room while her aunt adjusted the end of her veil for the sixth time, certain that a seventh adjustment would make the fabric behave better than the previous six. Outside, children ran between the legs of musicians. A boy had stolen two sweets and then returned one because his conscience had become difficult to carry with both hands full.

The grandmother, whose name was Sushila, examined the tray prepared for the family's prayers.

“Where is the other lamp?”

“On the shelf,” her daughter said.

“Why?”

“Because there are already three.”

“There should be four.”

“Mother, the procession is arriving.”

“Then it will have to arrive among people who know how to count.”

Her daughter fetched the lamp.

A distant burst of music entered the house. The family had lived in Jodhpur for three generations, but Sushila still spoke of Mandore as though one could reach its old stones by turning a corner inside the house. Her grandparents had told her of the place where Mandodari, daughter of their land, was said to have married the king of Lanka. The men of their community had recited the names of those believed to have accompanied his marriage procession. The story changed in the telling; the belonging did not.

Some members of the family claimed descent from that old company of travellers. Sushila's husband had believed it with an enthusiasm that grew whenever someone questioned him. She herself had never known what to make of an ancestor whose wedding lay so far beyond the reach of any family register. But she had watched the old men mourn Ravana after the city's effigies burned. She remembered her husband's father coming home from the prayers, smelling of smoke and sandalwood, and quietly refusing the sweets that other households offered in celebration.

The bride appeared in the doorway.

“Grandmother, they're here.”

“Then stand still. You are wearing half the family's jewellery and look prepared to run away with it.”

“I am prepared to get married.”

“That is what I said.”

The girl laughed, then saw the extra lamp.

“Is that for Ravana?”

Sushila nodded.

“Why do we honour him?” the girl asked. “My husband's family doesn't.”

“You must tell them we have better manners.”

“Grandmother.”

Sushila set down the tray. At the far end of the street, drums announced that the bridegroom's party had successfully discovered the correct house after considerable assistance from boys eager to be paid for directions.

“Mandodari came from here, our people say,” she answered. “The king of Lanka married her. Some families remember him as their own. Some say our ancestors came with his wedding party. Whether every part of that is as old as the story claims, I cannot tell you.”

“But he took Sita.”

“Yes.”

“Then why mourn him?”

The grandmother looked at the girl for a while. Around them, aunts and cousins had begun pretending not to listen.

“Because mourning is not a judgement that everything a person did was right,” she said. “If it were, there would be very few people to mourn.”

The bride considered this.

“Would you mourn Grandfather if he had done something terrible?”

“I mourned him when he spent all our savings on a pair of oxen that refused to work.”

The aunts laughed.

“That's different.”

“It is. But your grandfather was not made of separate men, one for each of his actions. I loved the man who brought me water when I was ill. I was furious with the man who bought those oxen. I buried one husband.”

She took the girl's hand and examined the red dye at her fingertips.

“Now go downstairs before they marry someone else in your absence.”

The girl kissed her grandmother's cheek. Sushila reached up to straighten the veil, although there was nothing wrong with it.

For a moment her fingers lingered against the girl's face.

Then the household swept her away towards the music.

That evening, when the bride had gone, Sushila returned to the room where the four lamps were burning low. She sat beside the empty tray and listened to the last of the guests leaving. Her husband's walking stick remained behind the door. Nobody had moved it in eleven years.

Across Jodhpur, another celebration would one day fill the sky with fire. In homes like hers, people would remember a different part of the same story. Sushila did not think the city cruel for celebrating a victory. She had never known how to reconcile the two customs, and no longer expected to.

She adjusted the fourth lamp before it went out.

“You would have argued with the bride,” she told the walking stick.

The stick gave her no disagreement.

She found that she missed it.

The invitation would not remain flat.

In Ravangram, in the Vidisha countryside, the bridegroom's younger sister had been entrusted with carrying it to the shrine. She was twelve, believed herself entirely suited to the task and had already bent the card twice while trying to keep it away from her little brother.

“Stop touching it,” her mother said.

“Then tell him to stop breathing on it.”

“Your brother is four.”

“That does not make his breath cleaner.”

The boy breathed upon the card again, delighted to have discovered a talent.

The family walked through the village towards the reclining image of Ravana. The figure lay on its back, long enough that the young children had once made a game of counting how many steps it took to pass from one end to the other. Some elders said the image must never be raised upright, because the village would suffer if it stood. Others spoke of the king as a learned ancestor, a Brahmin of the lineage their families claimed. Stories accumulated around the stone. Oil, flowers and the touch of familiar hands gave it the ordinary appearance of something that belonged to everyone who came there.

The bridegroom, Arun, had been silent most of the way.

His sister noticed.

“Are you afraid?”

“No.”

“You look afraid.”

“I am thinking.”

“Then you are afraid.”

His mother told her to be quiet.

At the shrine they placed the first wedding invitation near the reclining figure, according to the custom the family had followed for as long as anyone alive remembered. Arun bent to make his offering. His mother's lips moved in prayer. His little brother reached towards the oil and was rescued by a grandmother who had clearly anticipated the attempt.

Arun's sister watched the adults.

“In school they burn Ravana,” she said.

Her mother turned.

“Not in school.”

“Near school.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“Everybody goes.”

“Then everybody has a pleasant evening.”

“Why don't we burn him?”

The grandmother answered before the mother could.

“Because here we bring him invitations.”

“But why?”

“Because your grandmother brought them. And hers before her.”

“That isn't a reason.”

“It is a very old reason,” the grandmother said, “which is not always the same thing as a good one.”

The mother smiled despite herself.

Arun stood and moved aside so his sister could see the idol. She looked at its recumbent shape and wondered what sort of king required people to bend down to speak to him.

“Did he really bless marriages?” she asked.

“I don't know,” her mother said. “I know people come here hoping their children will be happy.”

Arun was looking at the invitation. He had written the bride's name upon it three times before deciding the letters were beautiful enough. He had wanted to marry her since they had shared a bench at a village lesson, when she lent him a pencil and then demanded it back before he had finished using it.

His sister elbowed him.

“You could still change your mind.”

He looked horrified.

“Why would I?”

“Because you have been looking as if someone has stolen your stomach.”

“That,” the grandmother said, “is what marriage feels like to a sensible man.”

The family laughed. Even Arun smiled, though the nervousness remained.

When they left, his mother took his hand briefly, as she had when he was small. He squeezed her fingers once before letting go. She did not attempt to hold him longer.

His sister walked behind them carrying the now-empty envelope. For all the stories she had heard about Ravana, the thing she remembered that afternoon was the tenderness with which her brother had spoken the bride's name when he thought nobody could hear.

The invitation remained at the shrine.

In other towns, a different image of the king would burn before the year was out. Here, a young couple had asked him to attend their beginning.

The boy had enjoyed the fireworks.

This was the first thing he admitted, and he admitted it with the apprehension of someone confessing to a crime whose punishment had not yet been agreed upon.

His grandfather sat on a low step outside their house in Paraswadi, in the Gadchiroli district, examining a damaged drum strap. He had promised to repair it before the community procession, and promises made to drummers were more dangerous to break than promises made to officials.

“The fireworks were good,” the boy said again.

His grandfather pulled a length of thread through the leather.

“I heard you.”

“And the effigy was very tall.”

“Good for the people who built it.”

“Ten heads.”

“Very inconvenient when buying hats.”

The boy laughed, then stopped.

“Everyone cheered when it burned.”

“Did you?”

“A little.”

The old man looked up.

“How much is a little?”

The boy spread his hands to the width of a small fish.

His grandfather looked at the measure gravely.

“That sounds serious.”

The boy's courage collapsed.

“Are you angry?”

“No.”

“But you say we honour Ravana.”

“We do.”

“Then I cheered when they burned someone we honour.”

The old man placed the strap across his knees. From the lane came the rattle of a motorbike and the laughter of women returning with bundles of cloth. The village was preparing its own observances. In Paraswadi, members of the Gond community placed a figure of Raja Ravana upon an elephant-shaped festival vehicle and carried him in procession. The songs were in Gondi; the drums carried farther than the words. Elders spoke of Ravana not as the defeated demon of the public play but as a revered ruler, a teacher, a figure belonging to their own account of who they were.

The boy had never liked being made to choose between people he loved.

At school his friends went to watch the effigy burn. At home his grandfather prepared to honour the same name. The boy wanted the fireworks and the drums, the sweet sellers and his grandfather's stories. He could not see why the world had arranged them on opposing sides.

“Why didn't you tell me?” he asked.

“Tell you what?”

“That the man in the effigy was ours.”

The old man was quiet for a moment.

“I told you his name.”

“You said he was a great king.”

“Yes.”

“You didn't say people would burn him.”

“I thought you knew.”

The boy stared at the ground.

“Grown-ups always think that.”

His grandfather accepted the rebuke.

“What did the teachers say?” he asked.

“That Rama defeated evil.”

“And what did you think?”

“I thought the fire was beautiful.”

The old man's face softened.

“It was beautiful?”

“Yes.”

“Then you saw beautiful fire. You weren't asked to decide the worth of a king.”

“But now I know.”

“Now you know that different people carry different things in the same name.”

The boy looked towards the covered festival figure in the courtyard.

“Which story is true?”

His grandfather drew the drum strap tight, testing the leather.

“I know the songs my mother taught me,” he said. “I know what our people have been called, and what it means to hear someone speak a name you were taught to honour as though it were only an insult. I cannot tell you what happened in a palace before any of us were born.”

“Then why sing?”

“Because not knowing everything is a poor reason to forget what people gave you.”

The boy considered this answer. He was old enough to suspect it concealed several more difficult ones, and young enough to be dissatisfied that his grandfather had not made the world easier.

Later, when the procession began, the elephant-shaped vehicle moved slowly through the village with Ravana's image held above it. Women sang. Children ran ahead and were called back. A wheel lodged against a stone, and six men argued about whether pulling or pushing was wiser. The drummers continued because rhythm did not require agreement.

The boy helped his grandfather steady the repaired strap. It held.

Across another part of the district, preparations for the evening burning continued. Both gatherings contained families, children, arguments over food, people eager to be seen and others who had come simply because their parents always had.

The boy looked at the king above the elephant, then at his grandfather, who was singing with his eyes closed.

He did not know whether he would cheer at the fireworks again.

He knew he would ask what they meant.

For now, he joined the song.

In Kanpur, the old door opened on a morning when much of the city was preparing for an ending.

The Dashanan shrine in the Shivala neighbourhood stood within the Kailash temple complex. Its doors remained shut through most of the year. On Vijayadashami, when Ravana's images would be burned elsewhere, worshippers came to honour the ten-headed king remembered for his learning and devotion to Shiva. They brought lamps and flowers. Some arrived before the streets were properly awake; others came after completing their prayers to Rama.

The priest, Harish, had been awake since before dawn. He had already misplaced his spectacles, recovered them from the shelf where he himself had put them, and instructed a young assistant not to confuse the vessels containing oil and water. The assistant had not confused them. This did not prevent the instruction being repeated.

A woman entered carrying her grandson and a small packet of flowers. The boy wanted to know why the temple opened only on this day.

“Because it is the day set aside,” Harish said.

“Why not tomorrow?”

“Because tomorrow it will be closed.”

The boy looked dissatisfied.

His grandmother gave Harish an apologetic smile. He returned one. Men who presided over rituals acquired an affection for questions that arrived before the crowds and less affection for the same questions at noon.

Near the shrine stood a young woman holding a cloth-covered case. She was due to perform at a small music gathering that evening and had come because her teacher spoke often of Ravana's mastery of the veena. He had told her that whatever one believed about the king's conduct, the tradition remembered him as a musician of formidable learning.

Her name was Meera. She had practised for fourteen years and still feared the first note of a performance more than any criticism afterwards. The case held a modern instrument, not the ancient veena of the stories, but the teacher had insisted that musicians belonged to a longer conversation than the particular instrument in their hands.

“Will you play?” the priest asked.

“Here?”

“Unless the instrument objects.”

Meera glanced at the waiting worshippers.

“I haven't warmed my hands.”

“They will warm.”

She took out the instrument and settled herself where the threshold opened towards the courtyard. Her first notes were uncertain. She stopped, adjusted a string and began again. The melody emerged slowly, without seeking the room's approval. A woman pouring oil into a lamp grew still. The assistant stopped arranging flowers. The boy leaned sideways to see the musician's hands.

Outside, someone tested a firecracker. The sudden crack entered the temple and made a child cry. Meera waited until the child had been comforted, then resumed at the place where the phrase had broken. She did not pretend the interruption had never occurred.

The boy approached his grandmother.

“Was Ravana really a great musician?”

“So they say.”

“And a scholar?”

“Yes.”

“And Shiva loved him?”

“That is in the stories.”

The boy looked up at the image. Ten crowned heads rose around the central face. Flowers lay before the shrine. The adults spoke of Ravana's knowledge, his strength, the blessings he received, the songs he offered in devotion.

“Was Ravana really that good?” the boy asked.

His grandmother smiled.

“Better.”

The priest heard her. He glanced towards the musician but said nothing. Meera had reached a passage in which the melody seemed to suspend itself just above the point where it must descend. The room waited for the next note.

At the far side of the courtyard, a man who had brought his daughter to see the unusual shrine listened to the exchange. He had come from another part of Kanpur, where the evening effigy was already being assembled. At home his daughter had been learning the story of Rama's victory. Here she watched people bow before the defeated king.

“Are they wrong?” she whispered.

The man looked towards the shrine. He had never worshipped Ravana and had no intention of beginning. He also knew that the boy's grandmother had come carrying flowers with the care he had seen his own mother bring to Rama's image.

“They are praying,” he said.

“But to Ravana.”

“Yes.”

“Then what should we do?”

“We came to see,” he answered. “So look.”

His daughter remained beside him until the musician finished. When Meera lifted her hands from the strings, the silence lasted longer than applause usually permitted. Then people began speaking again. A woman asked what she had played. Another asked whether she was married. Meera answered the first question and pretended not to hear the second.

By evening, the priest would close the shrine. Across Kanpur, enormous figures would flare against the dark, their painted faces breaking open amid cheers. The city would make room for both the prayers and the fire, though not everyone in it would approve of both.

The boy's grandmother took his hand and led him outside. He continued talking about the ten heads, and she continued answering until her patience was worn as thin as the cotton edge of her sari.

Meera wrapped her instrument. Her hands no longer trembled.

As she left, she saw a little girl standing at the gate, drawing the shape of a veena in the dust with the end of a stick. The girl had given it far too many strings.

Meera crouched beside her and showed her where one should go.

The girl listened, erased a line and drew it again.

On the other side of the sea, a father and daughter disagreed about a flying machine.

They were standing in a small exhibition in Sri Lanka, where a painted Ravana looked down over a display of landscapes, musical instruments and accounts of an ancient Lankan civilisation. Some panels spoke of his knowledge of medicine. Others presented him as a ruler of extraordinary technical achievement. At the end of the room, a golden aircraft crossed a painted sky above a city with towers finer than any the father had seen outside a picture.

His daughter, Nethmi, was seventeen. She had brought him because she was preparing a presentation at college and wanted to show him the exhibits before he began finding reasons to dislike them.

“Look,” she said. “They say Ravana could fly.”

“They say his machine could fly.”

“That's what I meant.”

“Then you should say it.”

Nethmi rolled her eyes.

Her father worked repairing electrical equipment. He believed in the capacity of machines to do almost anything that could be demonstrated twice, and was suspicious of those that required the audience to accept their greatness without seeing the workings. The golden aircraft offended him less as an old story than as a drawing without a place for anyone to sit comfortably.

“Where is the weight carried?” he asked.

“Appa.”

“Where is it?”

“It's a painting.”

“Then whoever painted it has been spared several difficult calculations.”

She laughed, though she had brought him partly because she knew he would say things like this.

They walked past an account of Ravana as an indigenous king, older than other stories of the island's beginnings. Nethmi read it with interest. She had grown up hearing fragments of these claims in songs, programmes, school conversations and messages shared by relatives who disagreed about almost every other part of the country's history. Some spoke of Ravana as a great ruler whose people had been misrepresented by outsiders. Others called him a figure from poetry and devotion, not a person to be placed inside a dated king-list.

“Why does it matter whether he was ours?” she asked.

Her father looked at her.

“What do you mean?”

“People get angry about him. They say he proves we were here first, that we were clever, that nobody taught us anything. They speak as though it changes who we are now.”

They stood before a map on which old place-names had been arranged into the shape of a lost kingdom.

“Do you want it to be true?” her father asked.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Nethmi took time to answer.

“Because it would be wonderful to think people here built extraordinary things before anyone wrote about us. And because everyone seems so certain about what we couldn't have done.”

He nodded.

“That is a better reason than a bad drawing of an aircraft.”

She smiled.

“But you think it's nonsense.”

“I think we should not have to invent greatness in order to believe our grandparents possessed it.”

She looked away. It sounded like the sort of line fathers saved for moments when their daughters had no immediate reply.

He added, more gently, “And I think stories sometimes point towards questions worth asking. If somebody says a king could fly, perhaps ask what people of the time knew about wind, cloth and wood. If somebody says a city was rich, ask what its people ate, how they moved water, what happened when a child became ill.”

“That's less exciting.”

“Only until you begin trying to build something.”

They returned to the painted machine.

Nethmi looked at the wing. The artist had covered it in gold. It was splendid and utterly without seams.

“Suppose there really was something,” she said. “Not this. Something that could fly for a little while.”

“Then I would like to know who made it.”

“Ravana.”

“Perhaps. And who cut the wood? Who shaped the frame? Who tested it when it failed? Who climbed inside?”

She looked at him, suddenly annoyed.

“You're trying to make the king ordinary.”

“No,” he said. “I'm trying to make room for everyone else.”

They wandered into the next room, where an old recording played a song about Ravana's devotion to Shiva. Nethmi's father listened until it finished. He recognised the melody from his mother's voice. She had sung it while folding washing, although she had always forgotten the third verse and invented new words to rescue the tune.

For a moment his expression changed.

Nethmi noticed.

“Did you know this song?”

“My mother did.”

“Can you sing it?”

“Not without doing it an injury.”

“Try.”

He refused, and she laughed, and he refused again. In the end he hummed three uncertain lines in a corner where nobody was listening closely. She recorded him on her phone before he could object.

When he discovered what she had done, he demanded that she delete it.

She promised she would.

Neither believed her.

Outside, the evening traffic moved slowly through the street. The young woman carried photographs of an impossible aircraft and a poor recording of her father humming a song he had learned from his mother. She would later discover that the photographs were easier to explain to her classmates.

The recording was the thing she listened to again.

The grandmother had forgotten the second verse.

This was a serious difficulty, because her granddaughter had been taught that songs possessed fixed words and that adults were supposed to know them. The grandmother disagreed with both propositions, but the child had brought home a school assignment and was in no mood for philosophy.

They sat beside an open window in an ordinary Indian home, where the evening sounds of a neighbourhood entered without invitation: a scooter being coaxed into starting, a vendor calling the last of his vegetables, a pressure cooker releasing steam in three offended bursts. The child had scattered notebooks across the floor. Her grandmother held a cup of tea that had cooled while she tried to remember why she had agreed to help.

“Rama defeats Ravana,” the girl read aloud. “Good defeats evil. We must explain why people celebrate Dussehra.”

“That is one explanation,” the grandmother said.

“It's in the book.”

“Books are very helpful. They also have the advantage of not having to answer questions.”

The girl looked up.

“Is it wrong?”

“No. It tells you what the festival means to many people.”

“But you told me Ravana was clever.”

“He is remembered as a scholar.”

“And he played music.”

“So the stories say.”

“And he loved Shiva.”

“Yes.”

“Then why was he bad?”

The grandmother adjusted her glasses.

“Because being learned does not make every choice wise.”

The girl thought about this with the expression of someone discovering that adults had once again complicated a task that should have fitted on one page.

“Did he really have ten heads?”

“I have never counted them.”

“Grandma.”

“I wasn't there.”

The girl laughed. The grandmother smiled into her tea.

On the table lay photographs the child had collected for the assignment: a blazing effigy; a carved king beneath Shiva's mountain; a reclining image in Ravangram; a picture of people bringing flowers to the Dashanan shrine in Kanpur. A school friend had sent another showing a Gond procession in Maharashtra. The child had arranged them in a row and was trying to make them agree.

“They are all Ravana,” she said.

“Yes.”

“They don't look the same.”

“People seldom look the same in other people's memories.”

“Which one is real?”

The grandmother examined the pictures. For a moment her fingers rested upon the blazing effigy. Then she moved to the photograph of a small shrine with flowers before it.

“I don't know what the man looked like,” she said. “And I don't know every reason people have for remembering him as they do.”

“Then how can I finish my assignment?”

“You can begin by saying what different people believe.”

The girl groaned.

“That will take two pages.”

“Your teacher will be delighted.”

“She said one page.”

“Then she will be less delighted.”

The child moved the pictures again, placing the burning figure next to the temple. She did not like the two images touching. After a moment she stopped trying to separate them.

“Did Ravana hurt Sita?” she asked.

The grandmother took off her glasses.

“In the story, he took her from her home against her will. That was wrong.”

“But some people say he never touched her.”

“Many tellings praise him for his restraint.”

“Then he was good?”

“Those are two different questions, darling.”

The child considered that. Her grandmother watched her, tempted to provide another explanation and deciding, for once, not to.

Somewhere outside, a boy was singing the refrain of a familiar festival song. He knew the melody better than the words and filled the gaps with syllables of his own invention. The child listened, then looked back at the photographs.

“Do you think Ravana would like the songs about him?”

The grandmother laughed.

“I think he would complain about the tuning.”

“Why?”

“Because people who love music always do.”

The child stood and went to a shelf where an old notebook lay beneath a box of photographs. She brought it to the window. Its pages had softened at the corners. The grandmother recognised the handwriting of her own mother and felt the small, abrupt ache of encountering someone who had been gone for so long in a gesture as ordinary as the crossing of a letter.

“Where did you find that?”

“You gave it to Mum.”

“I thought we'd lost it.”

“We didn't.”

The girl opened to a page containing a song. Some words had been corrected in darker ink. A line had been crossed out, then written again above it. In the margin someone had drawn a flower with too many petals.

“Is this the one?” she asked.

The grandmother leaned close.

“Yes.”

“Who wrote it?”

“My mother copied it from someone. I don't know who first made it.”

The child frowned.

“Then how do you know it's right?”

“I don't.”

“But you used to sing it.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

The grandmother looked at the open window, the evening light upon the floor, the child holding a book whose writer neither of them could name. She thought of her mother singing while her hands worked, stopping sometimes to laugh because she had forgotten a line. She had not remembered the woman's voice perfectly for years. Yet the first notes of the song returned with a painful ease.

“Because she sang it to me,” she said.

The girl sat beside her.

“Will you sing it now?”

“I told you I've forgotten the second verse.”

“Then we'll look at the notebook.”

“My mother changed the words.”

“Then we can change them back.”

“How will we know what they were?”

The girl considered the difficulty. She was beginning to enjoy it.

“We'll make something that fits.”

Her grandmother laughed, and the laugh caught unexpectedly in her throat. She set the cup aside before the child could see the tears gathering in her eyes.

“Come here,” she said.

The girl moved closer. Their shoulders touched. The grandmother began softly, the first line scarcely more than a breath. The child followed the marks in the notebook with her finger, then joined the melody at a place where the words became easy.

They reached the second verse.

The grandmother sang what she remembered.

The child stopped her.

“That's not what it says.”

“That's how my mother sang it.”

“Then your mother got it wrong.”

“Very likely. She was a talented woman.”

The girl laughed and began again. This time she sang the words on the page, while her grandmother sang the ones she remembered. They disagreed over the end of a line, lost the rhythm, found it again and continued, each refusing to surrender entirely to the other.

The song was not about a perfect king. Nor was it about a monster whose defeat made all other questions unnecessary. It contained a mountain, a musician, a prayer, and a name that had travelled through mouths and languages neither singer knew. Some of its images had been invented so long ago that invention was now called tradition. Others might have begun with something someone had seen and tried, imperfectly, to tell another person.

The girl did not ask where the song had begun. Not that evening.

She was occupied with persuading her grandmother to hold the final note properly.

“Longer,” she said.

“I am already singing it longer.”

“Not long enough.”

The grandmother smiled and drew a breath.

This time they reached the end together.

Then the girl turned back a page, found the first line, and asked to begin again.

The grandmother did.

Neither remembered the words exactly.

They sang anyway.
