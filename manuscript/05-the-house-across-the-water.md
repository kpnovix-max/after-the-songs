# Chapter Five

## The House Across the Water

*There were things the sea brought back. There were things it taught people to keep.*

The first time Analā heard someone speak of her uncle as a dead man, he was being remembered for a broken pot.

An old fisherman sat beneath Mēni’s breadfruit tree with a needle in his hand and a length of sail spread across his knees. He had come to repair a tear in the cloth Mēni used to keep rain off the cooking place. The tear was small. He had been repairing it for most of the morning because every few stitches someone came by with a question, and he liked questions more than sewing.

He was telling Ilā about a visit made years ago by the king of Lanka.

“Not a feast,” he said. “That's what people get wrong. We had no feast ready. We had fish drying on the racks and three jars of drinking water because the well had gone brackish. His ship came in with the tide. We thought they wanted something.”

“Did they?” Ilā asked.

“Water. A rope. A place to mend a steering pin. Ordinary things.”

“What did the king want?”

“He wanted to know why our landing steps were falling down.”

Ilā looked at the steps beyond the trees. Half the harbour used them now. The stones were broad and pale and worn smooth in the middle.

“They weren't like that then,” the fisherman said, following her glance. “The lowest one moved when you put your foot on it. He stood there with our headman and asked who had built it. Our headman said we had. The king asked whether we intended to drown our visitors before or after charging them for fish.”

Ilā laughed.

“He helped us haul the loose stone back into place, just enough to make the steps safe while we waited for proper repairs. A king, mind you. Had men who would have done it for him. He put his hands to the rope anyway. Then one of the boys ran to bring him a drink and dropped the cup. Broke the only painted one his mother owned. The king told the boy that if the cup had been made properly, it would have survived meeting the ground.”

“Did the mother believe him?”

“Not for a moment. She made him pay for another.”

The fisherman smiled at the memory. The needle went through the sail and came out again.

“It's the sort of foolish thing you remember,” he said. “And now he's dead.”

Analā was kneeling beside the cooking stones, holding a wet pot between her knees.

She had been trying to clean a ring of soot from its base. The shell scraper was still in her hand. She could see a narrow crescent of dark residue where the clay curved towards the rim. She had been looking at that mark with all the seriousness required of a difficult task.

She stopped scraping.

The fisherman pulled his thread tight.

“Who?” she asked.

He looked up.

“Child?”

“Who is dead?”

It was a stupid question. She knew the answer already. Perhaps she thought that if he said it again he would hear how absurd it sounded.

The fisherman glanced at Mēni, who was standing in the doorway with the baby asleep against her shoulder.

“No one has told her?” he asked.

Mēni did not answer quickly enough.

“Who?” Analā asked again.

The old man put down his needle.

“They say Ravana fell when the city was taken.”

The pot slipped out of her hands. It landed on the packed earth, rolled once and stopped against her ankle. It did not break.

For a moment this seemed important. The pot was whole. Its rim had not chipped. Mēni would not be angry.

“Who says?” Analā asked.

“Men from the coast. A sailor who came through the western islands. More than one.”

“You haven't seen him.”

“No.”

“You haven't seen the city.”

“No.”

“Then you don't know.”

The fisherman lowered his eyes to the needle in his palm.

“No,” he said. “I don't know with my own eyes.”

Mēni came down the step. The baby stirred, made a disapproving sound and settled again.

“Analā,” she said.

“People said the eastern wall was gone,” Analā replied. “It wasn't gone when we left. People said the harbour burned. We sailed through the harbour. They say things.”

“That is true,” Mēni said.

“Then why are you looking at me like that?”

Mēni sat on the earth beside her. She did not try to take the scraper away.

“I should have told you what people were saying,” she said. “I didn't know how.”

Analā looked at the sea, though from where she knelt she could see only a blue slit between two roofs.

“Did Sēnan know?”

Mēni rested a hand against the baby's back.

“Yes.”

Analā nodded once. It was the small, obedient nod she gave adults when they told her something that required her to behave well.

Then she picked up the pot.

“There's still soot,” she said.

She began scraping again.

***

That afternoon the sea rose higher than usual and swept the lower fishing nets sideways against the poles. A cloud passed in front of the sun. The whole island seemed to change colour for a moment: the leaves duller, the bright water turned to hammered tin, the white walls of the houses grey.

Analā worked until the ring of soot was gone. Then she washed the pot. Then she washed two bowls that were already clean. When Mēni told her there was nothing else to do, she said she had promised Ilā she would help sort the shells from a basket of sand.

Ilā had made no such request, but she fetched a basket.

They sat beneath the eaves, separating shells from bits of stone. The shells made a dry clicking noise as they fell into a shallow tray.

“Did you know?” Analā asked.

Ilā hesitated.

“I heard them talking.”

“Everyone knew.”

“I didn't know if it was true.”

“You could have told me.”

Ilā picked up a piece of coral, turned it over and placed it with the stones.

“I thought if it wasn't true, I would have made you sad for nothing.”

Analā wanted to tell her that she had been sad anyway. Instead she said, “You don't know what you're talking about.”

Ilā looked down.

They continued sorting.

After a while Ilā pushed the best shells into a neat little pile on Analā's side of the tray. There were five, each with a purple line running across the inside. Analā noticed but did not thank her.

She remembered her uncle giving her a piece of sugared fruit when she was small. Not a handful. One piece, lifted from a plate that had been set between adults. She had reached for another and he had drawn the plate towards himself with an expression of exaggerated alarm.

“A terrible danger,” he had said. “If I let you take two, every minister will ask for three.”

Her father had been at the table. He had laughed and told Ravana to stop encouraging her.

“Your father speaks as though he wasn't worse,” Ravana had said.

She could not remember what had happened next.

She could remember the exact colour of the fruit, amber at its edges, and the little granules of sugar that had stuck to her thumb. She remembered licking them off while the adults talked over her head. She remembered being certain that these people, whose voices she knew as well as she knew the rooms of her own home, would always be somewhere she could find them.

There had been nothing extraordinary about the afternoon.

That was what hurt.

She had not listened carefully enough. She had not known it was something she would someday try to recover. But she knew the last time she had seen him. She had been on the boat, turning towards the island, when she looked back and found him standing high on the western cliff above the burning city. The smoke had shifted and she had recognised his dark cloak. She had raised her hand to wave. Before she could know whether he had seen her, the boat had turned and the cliff was gone.

She had thought he would tell her father where she was. They were brothers. She had been so certain of that simple thing that she had lowered her hand without fear. Now she tried to remember whether he had moved, whether the dark shape on the cliff had lifted an arm in return. She could remember the smoke. She could remember her own fingers spread against the sky. She could not remember an answering hand.

She had seen him for the last time and had not known it was the last time. That was what she could not bear.

“Analā?”

Ilā was looking at her.

Analā realised that water was dripping from her chin onto the shell tray.

She wiped her face angrily with the back of her hand. The grains of sand caught on her skin.

Ilā said nothing. She moved the shells away from the wet patch and waited beside her.

***

In the days after the ship came, the islanders spoke more often of Lanka.

They did not speak of it as soldiers spoke of a place on a map. They spoke of it in the ordinary units by which people measure safety: the price of grain, the time a boat took to arrive, the weight of a rope, the number of days before a complaint received an answer.

An old woman said Lankan ships had once brought medicine when fever spread through the fishing quarter. The man who kept the storehouse said the harbour officials had honoured their measures; a jar marked for eight measures arrived with eight, not seven and a promise. A boatbuilder remembered a naval captain who inspected his work and paid him before sailing, though the work had not yet been finished.

“They had rules,” the boatbuilder said. “And if a captain broke one, you could complain above him.”

“Not always,” said the old fisherman.

“Most times.”

“You remember the officer who took your brother's timber without asking?”

“That was one officer.”

The fisherman threaded his needle without replying.

Analā listened from the shade of the storehouse. She was meant to be counting dry baskets, but every time someone mentioned Lanka she lost her place.

The islanders did not know every street in her city. They had not heard the morning lessons conducted in the eastern schools. They had not stood in the palace court when musicians tuned their instruments before a festival. They did not know how the stone grew warm beneath bare feet after the rain, or the smell of sesame oil from the little shop behind her mother's house.

But they knew something.

They knew that her home had been a place of people who made things and kept their word. They spoke of its sailors as men, not monsters. They remembered her uncle's visit as a story of a broken cup and an unpaid joke, not as the arrival of a terrible king.

She began sitting closer when they talked.

Sometimes she supplied a detail without being asked.

The naval officers did not all wear the same colour. There were different bands for harbour service and long voyages. A person carrying the king's seal did not simply enter a household. There were words to be spoken first. If someone was injured aboard a merchant vessel, a physician was called even if the injury belonged to a deckhand.

The islanders listened. A few corrected her. She corrected them back.

Once, when an older boy said her uncle had commanded every ship himself, Analā laughed before she could stop herself.

“He couldn't even command the musicians to finish on time,” she said.

Then she remembered.

Her laughter ended so abruptly that the boy stopped smiling too.

Later, she would remember that moment with particular shame, though she could not have explained why. She had laughed because she could still hear her uncle's voice. She had stopped because she believed she was no longer allowed to.

***

One evening Ilā found Analā on the edge of the harbour with a child's wooden boat resting between her knees. Someone had left it beside the steps with one side split open. Analā had found a thin splinter of wood and was trying to fit it across the break. The repair was going badly. Every time she pressed one end into place, the other lifted free.

“Your uncle had ten heads,” Ilā said.

Analā turned to her.

“Who told you that?”

“The men on the ship.”

“Which men?”

“The ones who came for the children. They were talking about it near the grain sacks. One said his commander had seen a picture. He said there were ten heads because Ravana was a monster.”

Analā stopped pressing at the splinter. It slid from her fingers onto the stone.

Analā stared at Ilā.

“He had one head.”

“I thought so.”

“Why did you ask?”

“Because you knew him.”

Analā looked away. Across the water the last light rested on the sails of fishing boats returning with their catches.

“People used to say he had ten minds,” she said at last.

Ilā considered this.

“Where did he keep the other nine?”

For the first time in days Analā almost smiled.

“It meant he knew many things.”

“Did he?”

“Yes.”

“Everything?”

“No.” Analā thought of the sugared fruit and the adults at the table. “He liked asking people questions.”

Analā fitted the splinter across the split again, holding it carefully with her thumbs.

“My father used to say that was the wisest thing about him,” she said. “Knowing whom to ask.”

Analā felt something lift inside her at the memory of those words. It was not happiness. It was closer to the relief of hearing a familiar song begin in a room where you had thought nobody knew it.

Ilā took the repaired boat and tried it in a puddle caught between the harbour stones. It tipped sideways and filled at once.

Analā sighed and took it back.

“The sea,” she said, “has opinions about my craftsmanship.”

Ilā laughed. Analā smiled despite herself.

It frightened her, how quickly ordinary life could return for a moment, as though nobody had died at all.

***

The merchant arrived six mornings later in a low vessel with two patched sails, a load of lamp oil and salt, and a man at the stern who kept asking whether anyone on the island had seen a particular fishing boat. Nobody had.

He had come from three ports to the west. The vessel had been delayed twice by poor winds and once by a harbour official who no longer recognised the seal on his trading permit.

The merchant was a narrow man with pale scars along his neck and the habit of rubbing his thumb against his first two fingers when he was uncertain. He spoke quickly when discussing prices and slowly when discussing anything else. He had not been to Lanka since before the fighting reached the city.

At first there was nothing unusual about his visit. He argued about the price of dried fish, complained about bad rope, and asked whether the island had enough oil to last the next month. Sēnan made him eat before they talked business. Mēni sent a bowl of lentils and flatbread to the storehouse, and the merchant declared that the island's cooks were the only people in the southern waters who did not ruin lentils with too much salt.

Only after the bowl was empty did Sēnan ask about Lanka.

The merchant wiped his hands.

“Taken,” he said. “That's the one part every port agrees upon.”

“By Rama?”

“By Rama's men. Or the forest army. Or some army that came with them. People don't agree on names. They agree that the old court is finished.”

“And the king?”

“Ravana is said to be dead.”

Sēnan looked at the empty bowl.

“Who governs now?”

“A governor. Some say a council. A man in the western harbour told me the northern prince installed a local ruler. Another said the same man was killed before his appointment could be announced. I wouldn't give you a copper for either account.”

“Did you speak to anyone from the palace?”

“No.”

“Anyone who saw the fighting?”

“One wounded sailor, though he claimed to have fought in three places that could not all be reached in the same day.”

Sēnan almost smiled.

The merchant reached for his cup, then set it down without drinking.

“There is something else.”

Sēnan waited.

“The king's brother crossed over before the end.”

“Which brother?”

“Vibhishana.”

The name seemed to take the air out of the small room.

The merchant noticed Sēnan's expression.

“You know him?”

“His name,” Sēnan said. “What does ‘crossed over’ mean?”

“Went to Rama's camp. Before the city fell. There are tales he told the northern commanders where the defences were weakest. Some say he argued against the war first and his brother wouldn't listen. Some say he was driven out. Some say he walked out because he wanted a crown.”

“What did he do after?”

“That's what I can't tell you.”

“Has anyone seen him?”

“Not anyone I've spoken with.”

“Could it be another man?”

The merchant shook his head.

“Four ports named him. All different men telling the story. Different reasons, different endings. The same brother.”

Sēnan sat very still.

He saw the girl at the landing steps when the children arrived: exhausted, too proud to admit she was hungry, waiting for someone to call the names of her brothers. He remembered the way she had stood when he asked her father's name, almost straightening as if saying it properly might bring the man nearer.

Vibhishana.

Sēnan had written it carefully.

“Do you know whether the children taken from here reached Lanka?” he asked.

The merchant blinked at the change of subject.

“What children?”

Sēnan described the recovery ship. A hundred and three children. The officer with the drums. The sacks of grain.

“I've heard nothing of them,” the merchant said. “But I haven't been to the mainland.”

Sēnan looked out through the doorway. Beyond the bright yard two children were trying to carry a water jar between them, each accusing the other of walking too quickly.

“Keep that story to yourself while you're here,” he said.

“Which story?”

“The brother.”

The merchant followed his gaze.

“Is there family here?”

“Children,” Sēnan said. “There are children here.”

The merchant nodded. He did not ask again.

***

Sēnan took out the register that night.

The tablet held three hundred and nine entries from the first arrival. One hundred and three names had been marked as departed aboard the recovery ship. Two hundred and six remained in the households of the island. He passed his finger over the first column, following names he had learned to pronounce and ages he no longer needed to look up.

He found Analā's name before he reached the middle.

Her father's name stood beside it.

It was the same mark he had made on the day she arrived. The mark had not changed. The world around it had.

He remembered a small hand held out to show him a necklace clasp, a child insisting she was fourteen and did not need to be spoken to like someone younger. He remembered her asking whether the boats would be reported to Lanka. He had told her they would.

Had he lied?

No. At the time he had believed every word.

He could call her to the storehouse tomorrow and tell her what the merchant had said. He could repeat the rumours and make her carry them alone. Or he could wait until someone came with a witness's account. He knew which choice would let her sleep another night.

He did not know which choice was right.

Before putting the tablet away, he touched the line where her father's name was written. It was only a name. He found himself wishing it belonged to someone else.

***

The house stood across a narrow reach of water from the inhabited shore, where the island curved inward and the sea became quiet enough to hold the reflection of the hills.

It was not a grand house. Its walls were white where the lime had held and the colour of old tea where rain had washed it thin. The roof sagged along one edge. A tree had grown through the yard and split the stone around its roots. Once, a trader had lived there with his family and stored weights, cloth and jars for voyages north. When his sons left the island, no one took the building. There was easier land closer to the harbour.

Analā found it while helping Ilā gather small crabs at low tide.

They had been arguing about which crab was the largest. Ilā said size ought to be measured from claw to claw; Analā said that would allow a small crab with unusually long arms to win. They were still debating it when they came over the ridge and saw the roof.

“Who lives there?” Analā asked.

“Nobody,” Ilā said. “The trader's family went away. Mother says the house is too far from everything. She says a person who builds a house here is asking the sea to do all the talking.”

They entered through a gap where the gate had fallen. Inside were a cracked water jar, two stones with holes drilled through their centres and the wooden frame of a weighing scale. Dust lay on the window ledge. A gecko fled across the wall at the sound of their feet.

The front room opened towards the water.

Beyond the narrow bay, the sea stretched northward. On a clear day, Ilā said, the far line of hills was visible for a few hours. Today the horizon was empty.

Analā stood at the window for a long time.

“This faces Lanka,” she said.

“Most of the island faces somewhere,” Ilā replied.

“No. I mean—” Analā stopped. “It faces home.”

Ilā came to stand beside her. There was room for both of them in the window, though they had to place their elbows one above the other.

“Is it the exact direction?” Ilā asked.

“I think so.”

“Then we should come when the sky is clear.”

Analā looked at her. Ilā was taking the matter seriously: the shape of the shoreline, the direction of the afternoon light, the possibility of seeing something. She was not pretending that Lanka was near. She was simply willing to help look.

Analā wanted suddenly to put her arms around her.

She did not.

Instead she said, “Your crab was bigger.”

Ilā considered this unexpected generosity.

“From claw to claw?”

“However you want to measure it.”

“Then it was much bigger.”

They stayed until the light fell across the floor in long pale strips. Neither saw the far hills.

***

Analā wrote the letter two days later.

She had asked Mēni for something to write on. Mēni found a piece of smooth bark that had been kept for household accounts and cut it carefully into a narrower strip. She gave Analā a little charcoal and pointed out where the fibres would catch if she pressed too hard.

Analā sat by the cooking place while Mēni fed the baby and Ilā practised tying a net knot that seemed determined to become a different knot every time she loosened it.

She began formally.

*Father,*

Then she could not think what to say.

For several minutes she looked at that single word. There were other words she knew, hundreds of them, and every one seemed to belong to a different sort of letter.

At last she wrote that she was safe.

She explained that they had reached an island where a man named Sēnan had taken down the children's names. She wrote that Mēni had a baby who pulled at her hair, and that Ilā was teaching her to work with rope, though neither of them was very good at it yet. She told him there had been a ship and that many children had gone away on it. She did not write about the officer's sword touching Sēnan's head. If she told him that, he would be frightened for her. She wanted him to come, not to imagine every terrible thing that had nearly happened.

She asked whether Arun had reached another shore. She said she had seen a boy with a wooden horse, but his horse had both ears, so it could not have been Arun's. She asked after Taranisena. She asked whether Mother was safe.

She did not know how to write her uncle's name.

She could ask whether her father had heard the stories. She could ask if they were lies. She could ask why no one had come to tell her. But if she wrote the question, she thought, it might make the answer more real.

So she left a space.

Then, below it, she wrote:

*Please come and find me.*

She held the bark near the light to see if the writing had smudged.

It was not a beautiful letter. The lines sloped. The charcoal became thick where she had stopped too long between words. But her father knew her writing. He had once told her that she made the same crooked curve on the last letter of her name every time. He would recognise it.

She asked Sēnan whether he could send it.

He read the first two lines and gave it back without looking farther.

“There may be a boat,” he said.

“To Lanka?”

“To one of the ports where someone might know how to carry it onward.”

“Will you try?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“When I know it won't be lost before it leaves the shore.”

She thanked him. Sēnan found that he could not meet her eyes.

He placed the letter in a dry box and told himself he would decide what to do in the morning.

***

She learned about her father three days later.

It happened beside the storehouse, at the hour when the afternoon heat made every conversation sound more quarrelsome than it was.

Two older children were sitting on an overturned boat, dividing a handful of roasted chickpeas. One of them had come from the western quarter of Lanka. His mother had worked near the fortress kitchens, and he knew enough about the palace to speak with the confidence of a person who had once seen its gates.

“My uncle says Ravana's brother gave the enemy the ways in,” he was saying.

“Your uncle wasn't there,” said the other.

“He heard it from a trader.”

“Traders hear everything after it happens.”

Analā stopped near the water jars.

“Which brother?” she asked.

The first boy looked up. His expression changed when he recognised her.

“Doesn't matter,” he said.

“Which brother?”

He looked at the other boy.

“Vibhishana,” he said.

A breeze lifted the loose edge of the storehouse awning. The cloth made a soft, repeated sound against its pole.

Analā heard the sound with extraordinary clarity.

“You're wrong,” she said.

“That's what they say.”

“He was away on business.”

The boy's face reddened. “I didn't say anything about you.”

“You said his name.”

“They say he went to Rama.”

“He went away before the fighting. That doesn't mean he went there.”

“I know.”

“Then don't say it.”

The second boy offered her a few chickpeas. She stared at his open hand as though it were an insult.

“Don't say it,” she repeated.

She walked away before either could reply.

She did not run until she was beyond the last house.

***

Sēnan was examining a cracked anchor weight when she found him.

He looked up at the sound of her footsteps and knew from her face that the decision he had postponed had been made for him.

“Is it true?” she asked.

He set the weight down.

“What have you heard?”

“Don't ask me that.”

“Analā—”

“Did my father go to Rama?”

Sēnan stood. He seemed older than he had a moment before.

“A merchant brought that report,” he said.

“When?”

“Several days ago.”

“And you didn't tell me.”

“I wanted to know whether it was true.”

“Did you find out?”

“No.”

She looked at him in disbelief.

“Then why didn't you ask the merchant?”

“I did.”

“What did he say?”

“That he had heard it in several ports. That he hadn't witnessed it. That people disagreed about why it happened.”

Analā took a step back.

“You knew his name.”

“Yes.”

“You knew he was my father.”

“Yes.”

“You let me write to him.”

Sēnan could not find an answer that did not make the silence worse.

“Where is the letter?” she asked.

“In the box.”

“Give it back.”

He went inside and returned with the bark wrapped in a cloth. She took it without thanking him.

For a moment she looked as if she might leave.

Then she asked, very quietly, “Did he help them kill my uncle?”

Sēnan felt the temptation to tell her no. He understood how easy the word would be. It would cost nothing to say it now and everything to take it back later.

“I don't know,” he said.

Her eyes filled. She blinked hard, furious with them.

“He loved my uncle.”

“I believe you.”

“They argued. Everyone argued. It doesn't mean—”

“I know.”

“My uncle used to tease him because he never liked the sweet things at supper. He'd put an extra piece on Father's plate just to annoy him.”

She stopped. Her mouth was open as though another detail were coming, but none came.

Sēnan waited.

“If he went there,” she said, “then he knew what they were doing to us.”

“That doesn't follow.”

“He knew they were coming.”

“Perhaps.”

“And he didn't come for us.”

“He may have tried.”

She looked at him with an anger so sudden that he almost flinched.

“You don't know,” she said. “You keep saying you don't know. Then why are you answering?”

Sēnan bowed his head.

“Because you asked me.”

She pressed the wrapped letter against her chest. For an instant she looked much younger than fourteen. Then she turned and walked away.

Sēnan did not follow her.

He thought of the day he had lied to the officer at the harbour. He had said a hundred and three when there had been three hundred and nine. He had chosen, in the space of a breath, which children would stay hidden. He had believed that a lie could sometimes be the kindest thing a person had to offer.

He had not expected kindness to become something a child might one day hate him for.

***

Analā went to the empty house.

She walked past the crab pools without looking at them. She crossed the ridge and pushed through the fallen gate. Dust rose around her ankles. The gecko vanished into the wall. Inside, the room was cooler than the path, and the window still faced north.

She sat on the floor with the letter across her knees.

For a long time she did nothing.

She thought of the ship that had come for the children. She thought of the officer's smile and the drumbeat that made his men sway as if they were celebrating something she could not see. She thought of Sēnan saying a hundred and three, his voice breaking over the number. She thought of her uncle at a table, defending a plate of sugared fruit from a small girl. She thought of her father laughing beside him.

All the memories were true. She did not know how they could belong to the same world.

She unfolded the cloth.

The bark was slightly curved now. She ran her fingers along its edges, smoothing it against her knee.

*Father,*

She stared at the word until the charcoal began to blur.

She wanted to ask why.

She wanted to ask whether the merchant was a liar. She wanted her father to be standing in the doorway, ready to tell her that there were things she did not understand, and that she could be angry with him after supper, once he had found food and somewhere for them to sleep.

She wanted him to come.

That was the worst part.

She turned the letter over so that she could not see what she had written.

The sea moved through the bay, touching the stones one by one. Far off, someone called to a fishing boat. The sound crossed the water and faded.

After a while she turned the bark back again.

At the bottom were the words she had written so carefully:

*Please come and find me.*

She picked up the charcoal she had carried in her pocket. It was broken in two. She held the larger piece above the line, but her hand would not move.

The words had been written by a girl who believed her father was looking for her.

She did not know whether that girl had been foolish.

She knew only that she could not bear to be her anymore.

Very slowly, she drew a line through *Please*.

Then through *come*.

Her hand stopped before *find me*.

The charcoal left a black smudge where her thumb pressed against it.

She heard feet outside. Ilā appeared in the broken doorway carrying a small bowl of rice and a folded cloth.

“Mēni said you hadn't eaten,” she said.

Analā turned her face towards the window.

Ilā did not ask what had happened. She came inside, set the bowl on the floor and lowered herself beside the wall. They sat without speaking while the light faded from the sea.

After a time, Ilā pushed the bowl closer.

“The rice is going hard,” she said.

Analā managed a small, broken laugh. It turned into a sob so quickly that neither of them seemed prepared for it.

Ilā moved towards her, stopped, then put her arms around her.

Analā tried to speak. She wanted to say that her uncle was dead, that her father might have helped the men who killed him, that her mother had promised to follow, that she did not know where Arun was, that the whole world had changed while she had been busy learning how to make rope. She wanted to explain that she had loved them all at the same table and that no one had warned her love could be divided into sides.

No words came.

She cried against Ilā's shoulder until the cloth was wet and her throat hurt. Ilā held her without asking her to stop.

When the crying eased, Ilā said, “You can stay here tomorrow too, if you want.”

Analā looked at the darkening water.

“Here?”

“On the island,” Ilā said. “With us.”

Analā closed her eyes. She thought of the house she had left, the people who might never return to it, the father she might no longer want to see. Then she thought of Mēni waiting with the baby, of Ilā carrying rice across the ridge without being asked, of the little wooden boat she had tried to mend, sinking as soon as Ilā set it in the water.

“I don't know what I want,” she said.

“That's all right,” Ilā replied. “We can go home first.”

Home.

Ilā stood and held out her hand.

Analā took it.

She left the letter beneath the broken weighing scale, where the sea air could not reach it easily. She told herself she had put it there because she didn't want to carry it back.

At the doorway she turned once to look at the window.

Beyond it lay a country where her uncle was dead and her father, somewhere, was alive.

She did not know whether she wanted him to find her.

She did know that if he came tomorrow, she would have to decide whether to run towards him or away.

Ilā squeezed her hand gently.

They went down the path together, towards the small house where Mēni had left a lamp burning.
