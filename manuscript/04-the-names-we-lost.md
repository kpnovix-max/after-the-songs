# Chapter Four

## The Names We Lost

*Not everyone who disappears is gone.*

The first man through the door wanted his brother released.

The second wanted the first man arrested.

Behind them, a woman was demanding a cart, two physicians, and the return of her husband's body, though she had not yet been told whether he was dead.

Vibhishana listened to all three.

Beyond the open windows, someone was beating a drum.

“Majesty,” said the first man, “my brother was taken from his own house.”

“Your brother was carrying a sword,” said the second.

“He was carrying it to the smith.”

“At night?”

“The smith works at night now. Everyone works at night. There is no food in the day.”

The woman pushed between them.

“My husband is in the southern hospital. Or the eastern hospital. They told me both.”

Vibhishana turned to the clerk on his right.

“Send someone to both.”

“We have, Majesty.”

“Then send someone who will return with an answer.”

The clerk made a mark on his tablet.

A crash came from the courtyard. Somebody shouted. The drumbeat stopped for a moment and resumed.

The second man began speaking again.

Vibhishana held up a hand.

“Did you see the sword?”

“No, Majesty. But the soldiers said—”

“Then you did not see it.”

“No.”

He looked at the first man.

“Your brother's name?”

The man told him.

“To the holding house,” Vibhishana instructed the clerk. “Have the arresting officer identify the charge. If there is none, release him before sunset.”

The first man bowed.

The second protested.

The woman began to cry.

Vibhishana had been king for twelve days.

***

The palace had survived the war better than the city around it.

That was what visitors said.

The roofs were mostly intact. There were still polished floors, carved pillars, servants who knew where to stand, and a throne large enough to make a man feel foolish sitting upon it.

But the eastern kitchens had no oil. The northern cistern was contaminated. Half the records from the treasury had been burned, and the other half contradicted one another. Two sets of guards claimed responsibility for the west gate. Neither had guarded it the previous night.

The king's orders travelled through corridors crowded with messengers and returned, when they returned at all, carrying explanations.

There were too few carts.

There were too many wounded.

The bridge was down.

The officer had been reassigned.

The seal was not recognised.

Someone had demanded payment.

Someone else had threatened the messenger.

A clerk entered with a basket of tablets and placed it beside Vibhishana's chair.

“Urgent matters, Majesty.”

“The previous basket was urgent.”

“These arrived after it.”

“Does that make them more urgent?”

The clerk hesitated.

“I don't know, Majesty.”

“Neither do I.”

The clerk looked so miserable that Vibhishana regretted the words.

“Put them here.”

He opened the first tablet.

Grain seized by armed men outside the western storehouse.

The second concerned a child found beneath a collapsed stairway.

The third was a complaint from a Rama commander about the quality of the lodging provided to his soldiers.

The fourth reported an outbreak of fever.

The fifth was a request for permission to bury unidentified bodies.

He set the tablets in separate piles. The piles immediately became confused when a messenger brought another six.

Outside, the chanting grew louder.

“Jai Shri Ram!”

A second group answered from farther down the street.

Vibhishana had heard those words spoken with reverence by men who had risked their lives to bring water to the wounded. He had also heard them shouted by men overturning a fruit seller's stall.

The words did not tell him what the men were doing.

He needed to know what they were doing.

“Send a patrol to the market,” he said.

“Lankan or allied, Majesty?”

“Both.”

“The Lankan patrol is not permitted to carry weapons beyond the palace quarter.”

“Then send them without weapons.”

“And if the crowd is armed?”

Vibhishana stared at the clerk.

The clerk lowered his eyes.

“Ask the allied commander for an escort,” Vibhishana said.

The clerk wrote it down.

A king, Vibhishana was discovering, could ask for many things.


Lanka had lost its army before it had lost the war.

The units that had survived the last fighting had scattered. Some soldiers had gone home, some had thrown away their insignia, and some had become indistinguishable from the armed men now roaming the streets. The navy was worse. Ships had been burned, captured or driven away; the remaining crews were too few and too frightened to form a fleet. There were Lankan guards at the palace, but they could scarcely protect the palace, let alone a city.

Rama had known this. Before departing, he had promised nine months of military assistance while Vibhishana restored the kingdom's own forces and institutions. The offer had been freely made. No tribute was demanded in exchange. Rama's written instructions required his commanders to protect civilians, respect the new king's authority and help restore ordinary life.

Vibhishana believed those instructions were sincere. He had heard Rama speak to frightened Lankan families without contempt. He had watched him rebuke a soldier who called all the city's inhabitants enemies. When a shipment of grain had been delayed, Rama himself had ordered it released.

But Rama was no longer in the room. His commanders were.

The nine months had begun. Vibhishana was counting the days for two different reasons: he needed the soldiers to remain, and he needed to learn how to rule without them.


The doors opened before the next petitioner had been called.

Two allied commanders walked into the audience chamber. The palace attendant followed them, red-faced.

“Majesty, I told them you were receiving—”

“It's all right,” said the taller commander, who had not asked Vibhishana. He was eating a piece of fruit. “This will only take a moment.”

The petitioner stepped aside. Neither commander bowed.

“We'll retain the northern barracks,” the taller man said.

“I ordered them cleared for the wounded.”

“We saw the order. Our men need somewhere to sleep.”

“There are empty houses near the gate.”

“Empty houses with broken roofs. We aren't moving the men there.”

The second commander unrolled a tablet. “And the harbour patrols remain under our authority. You requested two vessels for grain deliveries. Those vessels aren't available.”

“They are Lankan vessels.”

“Of course, Majesty.”

The man spoke as if agreeing with a child about the colour of the sky.

Vibhishana could have called the palace guards. He knew exactly how many stood outside. He also knew how many soldiers the two commanders could summon from the northern barracks. More importantly, he knew that those soldiers were keeping armed gangs away from the hospital.

“Give me a date for returning the barracks,” he said. “And release one vessel by tomorrow morning. The grain is already rotting on the quay.”

“We'll see what can be arranged.”

“I would prefer an answer.”

“You shall have one.”

They turned to leave. The taller man tossed the fruit stone into a bronze basin that had once held ceremonial flowers. He missed. Neither man noticed.

The attendant waited until their footsteps faded.

“They didn't even ask permission to enter.”

“No.”

“They cannot keep doing this.”

“They can,” Vibhishana said. “For the moment.”

“You are the king.”

“I am.”

“Then why let them?”

“Because tonight I need their soldiers at the western gate.” Vibhishana bent to retrieve the fruit stone. “We must choose which battles we can afford.”

He dropped it into the basin.

“Bring back the petitioner.”

***

His son had been found in a drainage chamber beneath a ruined house.

For almost twenty hours Taranisena had crouched in water that reached his thighs. He had kept one hand over his mouth because the men passing above him were shouting and he did not know whose men they were. The water had been cold. Once, when a stone fell through a crack overhead, he had swallowed a cry and bitten his own hand hard enough to draw blood.

He had heard footsteps, breaking wood, and a woman calling a name.

He did not know whether it was his mother's voice.

He had not answered.

When the rescuers lifted him out, he fought them until someone said his father's name.

Now he slept in a chamber with a dry floor, a clean bed and a lamp that burned through the night.

He was old enough to know that the lamp was wasteful.

He was young enough to need it.

That morning, before the petitions began, Vibhishana had gone to see him.

Taranisena was sitting on the floor beside the bed, eating bread without appetite. A clean strip of cloth covered the hand he had bitten while hiding; he kept it close to his chest when anyone came near.

“Did you sleep?”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“I don't know.”

Vibhishana sat on the edge of the bed.

“Shall I have them bring you something warm?”

“No.”

“Have you spoken to the physician?”

“Yes.”

“What did he say?”

“That I should sleep.”

Vibhishana looked at the untouched blankets.

After a while Taranisena asked, “Have they found Arun?”

“Not yet.”

“And Analā?”

“Not yet.”

His son tore a small piece from the bread.

“Mother?”

Vibhishana could not give him another answer.

He had seen her body. The physicians had identified her. There was no room for hope in that particular grief.

Taranisena stared at the floor.

“If you're king now,” he asked, “why can't you find them?”

Vibhishana opened his mouth.

From the corridor came the sound of someone running.

“Majesty!”

He looked at his son.

“I am trying.”

Taranisena nodded, not because he understood, but because he could see his father needed him to.


Two days later a sealed message arrived from Rama.

It confirmed that the allied forces would remain for the promised nine months, that supplies had been allocated to the city's hospitals, and that Vibhishana's orders concerning the protection of civilians were to be obeyed. The wording was clear, respectful and, to Vibhishana, unmistakably Rama's.

He felt relief so sharp that for a moment he had to sit down.

Then he sent a copy to the commander responsible for the hospital carts.

The answer came back before supper. The carts could not be released until the commander had completed his own inventory.

Vibhishana read the reply twice. He did not doubt Rama's generosity. He doubted that generosity could travel through six officers, three depots and a city full of frightened men without becoming something else.

That evening an allied captain brought the first two carts himself. He apologised for the delay, unloaded the jars with his soldiers and asked whether the hospital needed more.

Vibhishana thanked him by name.

He was learning to remember the names of the men who helped.

***

By noon Vibhishana had settled a dispute over grain weights, ordered the reopening of three wells, and persuaded an allied captain to release seven men arrested without charges.

He had failed to secure carts for the eastern hospital.

He had failed to persuade the northern garrison to surrender a store of medicine.

He had failed to find Arun and Analā.

The last failure did not appear on any of the day's official reports.

Their names were written on a small tablet he carried inside his tunic.

He had written them himself, although every clerk in the palace knew how to write.

**Arun. Analā.**

Below them he had written five places.

The family house.

The western breach.

The temporary shelters.

The harbour.

The burial grounds.

At the house, the searchers had found bodies, scorched cloth and a child's toy. A wooden horse with a damaged ear had been recovered among the debris, though no one could agree which room it had come from. One witness said two children had burned there. Another swore she had seen a boy and a girl running towards the city wall.

At the western breach, a soldier remembered a little boy. He thought the boy had been alone.

At the shelters, a clerk had entered the name Arun twice and crossed it out once. He could not explain why.

At the harbour, a boatman remembered children being taken aboard after the lists were sealed. He could not remember their names.

At the burial grounds, a keeper had recorded three small bodies brought from the palace quarter. One had been identified as Analā because of a toy found nearby. The identification was not certain. The burial had already taken place.

The reports did not fit together.

Vibhishana had asked for each search to be repeated.

Some had been repeated. Some had not.

He knew the difference.

He also knew that, in the hours spent arguing over carts and wells and grain, he had not once asked whether anyone had gone back to the harbour.

He hated himself for noticing that only now.

A messenger appeared.

“The western storehouse, Majesty. The crowd has returned.”

Vibhishana put the tablet inside his tunic.

“Who is commanding the patrol?”

The messenger named a man Vibhishana had dismissed two days earlier.

“Of course,” Vibhishana said.

He rose.


He had searched five places, or rather he had ordered five places searched and had gone himself to two before being called away. At the family house he had stood among the ashes until a guard asked him to leave because a wall was about to fall. At the harbour he had questioned a boatman for half an hour, only to learn that the man had arrived two days after the evacuation.

There were too many witnesses and too few witnesses worth trusting. Some had seen what they said they saw. Others were repeating stories in the hope of being useful, or being paid, or being left alone. The records were worse. A name written in one shelter could refer to three people; a name omitted from a list might mean that no one had thought to ask it.

He knew how a proper search ought to be conducted. He knew it was not being conducted. He could not find enough people to repair the wells, let alone send trained clerks to every place where his children might have passed.

***

Over the following days, thirteen requests for evacuation or recovery reached the king in a single morning.

They arrived from different parts of the city and beyond it.

Families trapped near the eastern gate. Workers beneath the collapsed kilns. Civilians hiding in an old granary. Children stranded across the northern canal. Patients who needed to be moved from the southern hospital. Children evacuated before the fighting to an island beyond the coast. Families in the western salt warehouses. Fishing households cut off by damaged roads. Palace servants sheltering in a cellar. Survivors beyond a destroyed bridge. Prisoners whose names had not been recorded. People trapped in a quarry. Children and elderly people hiding in a monastery.



The clerk had numbered the requests so that they would not be lost. The island was sixth. Vibhishana did not know the island's name; he knew only that children had been sent there before the fighting and that someone had promised to bring them back. There were other such promises, made in other desperate rooms by people who might now be dead.

He signed all thirteen authorisations. The ink was barely dry when a messenger returned the first: no escort available. Then the second: vessel requisition denied. The clerk stacked the refusals beside the requests, and the stack began to lean.

The clerk read the requests aloud.

Vibhishana interrupted him three times to ask about food, twice about water, and once because he thought he recognised a name among the missing.

It was not Arun.

“Which of these need ships?” he asked.

“Three, Majesty. Possibly four.”

“Which need armed escorts?”

“Most.”

“Which can wait?”

The clerk looked at the tablets.

“None of them say they can wait.”

Vibhishana rubbed his eyes.

“Of course they don't.”

He went to the allied commander's quarters.

The commander received him in a room that had once belonged to the palace's keeper of music. Two instruments lay broken against the wall. A painted ceiling showed musicians seated beneath flowering trees.

The commander was courteous.

He offered Vibhishana water and a chair.

Then he refused the request for soldiers at the northern canal.

“We have too few men, Majesty.”

“There are children on the far bank.”

“There are armed men in the city.”

“Then give me a boat and a guide.”

“I cannot spare a boat.”

Vibhishana set down the tablet.

“You have four boats at the southern quay.”

“Three are needed for supply.”

“And the fourth?”

“Held in reserve.”

“For what?”

“For emergencies.”

Vibhishana looked at him.

The commander sighed.

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

After a long silence the commander agreed to release the fourth boat.

The second request went worse.

The families at the salt warehouses were beyond a district where armed groups had begun stopping travellers and demanding proof of loyalty. The commander refused to send a patrol.

“Those streets are not secure.”

“They will never become secure if no one enters them.”

“I will not lose men for a few dozen families.”

Vibhishana leaned forward.

“You have asked me to govern this city. Help me make that possible.”

The commander studied him.

At last he said, “I'll send a captain to assess the route. No promises.”

It was not enough.

Vibhishana thanked him anyway.

They turned to the remaining requests.

For the island, a cargo vessel was available. A junior officer could command it. The ship would carry provisions and bring back any children still there.

“Good,” Vibhishana said.

He was already reaching for the next tablet.



The junior officer had been chosen because he was free, not because anyone believed him especially suited to children. The allied commander regarded the assignment as a routine cargo run. Vibhishana regarded the release of a ship as one problem, out of thirteen, temporarily solved.

He had never met the officer. He did not know the man drank, or how he behaved when drunk, or what he believed a victory entitled him to do.

Before the meeting ended, another messenger arrived with news that the northern canal boat had run aground. The commander began arguing about who would pay for its repair. The island vessel was already forgotten.


***

That afternoon, Vibhishana found the western storehouse surrounded by men carrying sticks and flags.

Some were demanding grain. Some were demanding that the storekeeper be punished for refusing grain. Others had come to watch.

A group of allied soldiers stood at the entrance.

They were not stopping the crowd.

One of them was laughing.

Vibhishana stepped from his carriage.

A young officer saluted him. The salute was correct, but it arrived several moments late.

“Majesty.”

“Why is the grain not being distributed?”

“We're waiting for authorisation.”

“I authorised it this morning.”

“From our command, Majesty.”

Vibhishana looked at him.

“This is Lanka's grain.”

“Yes, Majesty.”

“In Lanka's storehouse.”

“Yes.”

“Intended for Lankan families.”

“Yes.”

“Then why must your commander authorise its distribution?”

The young officer swallowed.

“Our orders are to secure the stores until the city is stable.”

“Does a starving crowd make the city stable?”

The officer said nothing.

Vibhishana took a breath.

“Open the doors. Let the storekeeper measure the grain. Your men can guard the line.”

“I'll have to ask, Majesty.”

“Then ask quickly.”

The officer hurried away.

A woman in the crowd shouted that her children had not eaten since yesterday.

Another shouted that the new king had given the grain to Rama's men.

Someone else shouted that Vibhishana had sold the city.

The words struck him harder than he expected.

He turned towards the crowd.

“The grain is yours,” he called. “You will have it.”

A man raised his stick.

“Then why are we waiting?”

Vibhishana had no answer he could give from the steps of a storehouse.

The officer returned with permission.

The doors opened.

The crowd surged forward.

For a few minutes there was danger. Then the storekeeper began measuring, the soldiers formed a line, and the shouting changed from anger to impatience.

Vibhishana stayed until the first families received their grain.

One of the allied soldiers approached him.

“My lord,” he said quietly, “there are men in the crowd carrying knives. We should take them aside before somebody is hurt.”

The soldier was young and spoke with respect.

Vibhishana nodded.

“Do it carefully. No beating. No public humiliation.”

“Yes, my lord.”

The soldier went.

Vibhishana watched him separate two men from the crowd without drawing his weapon.

There were still men who understood what an order was for.

He wished there were more of them.

***

There was a corridor in the palace where Ravana had once entertained visiting musicians.

It had been built to carry sound.

Even a single plucked string could be heard clearly from one end to the other. The walls were painted with dancers, scholars, astronomers, poets and the kings who had supported them.

Vibhishana had walked through it beside his brother many times.

Once Ravana had stopped beneath a painting of a veena player and argued with the artist over the position of her fingers. The artist had disagreed. Ravana had demanded an instrument so that they could settle the matter.

The argument had lasted an hour.

Now the corridor smelled of spilled liquor.

A new painting covered part of the eastern wall.

A figure with ten heads towered over a burning city. Its mouths were full of teeth, its eyes yellow and furious. Beside it stood a green woman with a hooked nose and long claws. Behind them rose an enormous creature with a belly like a hill.

Ravana.

Their sister.

Kumbhakarna.

Vibhishana stopped.

He could still see the edge of an older painting beneath the fresh pigment. A musician's hand emerged from behind the monster's shoulder.

A drum sounded.

Two soldiers were dancing beneath the caricature.

They faced each other with their arms outstretched, curved around one another without touching. Their knees bent. Their hips moved in slow, elaborate circles, coming forward and withdrawing with each heavy beat.

Their faces were fierce.

They stared into each other's eyes with an intensity that seemed almost violent.

Then the rhythm turned.

Their eyes closed.

Their mouths opened into broad, dreamy smiles, as though the world had become entirely beautiful and nothing in it could trouble them.

Another turn.

Eyes open.

The smiles vanished.

The fierce gaze returned.

They circled again, grown men rolling their hips with the grave concentration of children inventing a game. One had a sword at his waist. The other had tucked a strip of saffron cloth into his belt. They moved so close that their foreheads nearly touched, then drew apart, arms still stretched wide.

The drummer struck harder.

The men closed their eyes again and smiled.

Someone cheered.

Vibhishana watched the movement repeat.

He had seen celebrations before. He had attended them with his brother. He knew that victory made people foolish.

But there was something in the solemnity of the men's expressions, the way they passed so easily from menace to pleasure, that made him cold.

A general came up beside him.

“Majesty. You shouldn't trouble yourself.”

Vibhishana pointed towards the wall.

“Who painted that?”

The general glanced up.

“Some of the men. It was done in good spirits.”

“They've painted my brother as a beast.”

The general smiled.

“Let the boys celebrate. Young blood.”

Neither dancer was young.

One had grey in his beard.

Vibhishana looked at the green figure beside the ten-headed monster.

“And my sister?”

The general's smile thinned.

“Your family stood against Lord Rama.”

Vibhishana said nothing.

The drums continued.

“I assure you,” the general added, “Lord Rama has given the clearest instructions that your kingship be respected. He wishes Lanka restored. We all do.”

“I believe he does.”

“Then let the men enjoy the victory. It will pass.”

Vibhishana looked at the painting again.

His brother had once argued with an artist beneath that very wall.

“Will it?” he asked.

The general did not hear him.

The dancers turned.

Eyes closed.

Smiles.

Vibhishana walked on.

***

He was not certain when the reports about his family had begun to contradict one another.

Perhaps they had always contradicted one another.

The first account said the household had burned and everyone inside had died.

Then Taranisena had been found alive.

A second account said Arun had escaped with servants.

A third placed Arun near the western wall.

A fourth claimed that a boy of his age had drowned near the harbour.

For Analā there were equally certain accounts: burned in the house, seen running with a woman, carried aboard a boat, taken towards the shelters, found among the dead.

Every witness remembered something.

Nobody remembered enough.

The household register recorded Analā as dead. Vibhishana had authorised the funeral because the officials had placed the evidence before him and because, at the time, he had not known how to argue with it.

Later he had learned that the toy used to identify the remains had been found in a room where several children had sheltered.

The certainty had begun to unravel. Vibhishana had ordered the death entry marked as disputed and the search reopened. The funeral could not be undone. Neither could the fact that no one had identified her with certainty.

But an unravelled certainty was not the same as proof of life.

One afternoon a junior clerk brought him an account from a boatman who had helped load children before the city fell.

The man remembered a woman arriving after the passenger list had been sealed.

She had been well dressed, though her clothes were torn.

She had argued with the attendants.

She had insisted that a girl be allowed aboard.

The boatman had not heard the girl's name.

“Was the woman my wife?” Vibhishana asked.

“We don't know, Majesty.”

“Did he describe her?”

“He said she was tall.”

“Half the women in Lanka are tall.”

“Yes, Majesty.”

“Did he see where she went afterwards?”

“No.”

“Did he see the girl leave?”

“He remembers the girl being lifted aboard. He cannot say which boat.”

Vibhishana stared at the clerk.

“Find him.”

“We have sent for him.”

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

“And?”

“He has gone south. His village was damaged. No one knows where he is staying.”

Vibhishana closed his eyes.

For a moment he saw his wife standing at the harbour, one hand gripping Analā's wrist, refusing to let the attendants turn them away.

It was possible.

It was exactly the sort of thing she would have done.

He wanted to believe it so fiercely that he distrusted himself.

“Find the boatman,” he said.

The clerk bowed.

“Majesty, the eastern granary—”

“Yes,” Vibhishana said.

He rose.

The boatman's account was copied onto a tablet and placed among the unresolved witness statements. It was not attached to any evacuation register. No clerk knew which vessel to search.

The boatman would not be found that week.

***

The truth was smaller than all the reports.

On the day the boats left, Analā's mother had come to the harbour after the lists were sealed.

She had not come to evacuate herself.

She had come to put her daughter aboard.

The attendants had told her the passenger record was complete. The boats were full. There were procedures. There were orders.

She had listened.

Then she had pushed past them.

She knew the chamberlain's people. She knew which officer had been told to obey which seal. She knew that if she waited for permission, the boats would leave.

She had found a place for Analā.

She had promised to follow.

She had not asked anyone to add the girl's name to the official list.

There had been no time.

It was the last thing she had done for her daughter.

And it was why, in Lanka, the list of children evacuated did not contain Analā's name.

The island register did.

The two records would never be placed side by side.

***

Weeks passed. Some streets grew quieter; others did not. When the first reports from the recovery missions began to arrive, Vibhishana was trying to arrange the burial of bodies found beneath the northern barracks.

There were too many to identify.

A physician wanted them buried immediately.

A priest insisted the families be given time.

The commander responsible for the district wanted the road cleared.

A clerk read the reports aloud while Vibhishana listened to all three.

“Seventy-two recovered from the eastern kilns. Two hundred and eleven from the old granary. Forty-six brought across the northern canal. Thirty-eight patients transferred from the southern hospital. One hundred and three children recovered from the island. Two hundred and six reported missing before arrival. Twenty-seven released from the holding house—”

“Wait,” Vibhishana said.

The clerk stopped.

“The patients. Was clean water sent ahead of them?”

“Not yet, Majesty.”

“Then where have they been taken?”

“To the western ward.”

“The western ward has no clean water.”

The physician began to explain.

Vibhishana held up a hand.

“Send two carts of sealed jars now. If there are no carts, use the palace animals.”

The clerk made a mark.

He continued reading.



He had heard the number, and for an instant it had hurt him: two hundred and six children. Then a physician had said there was no clean water in the western ward, and the number had been pushed aside by another emergency. That was how the days worked. The suffering did not diminish. It merely waited its turn.

The clerk who compiled the returns had no reason to question the island report. A ship had brought back 103 children. Its officer had reported that 206 others were lost before reaching the island. The figure was entered in the column for missing or presumed dead. There was no separate column for children hidden from a dangerous crew.

The island report was placed beneath the others.

Later, while eating a bowl of rice that had gone cold, Vibhishana remembered a number.

Two hundred and six.

He could not remember whether it referred to the granary, the hospital or the boats.

He sent for the reports.

They did not arrive before the next interruption.

***

By the time the reports had been filed, the western grain store was threatened again.

The commander at the northern gate refused to release a patrol because his orders had not been countersigned.

A merchant complained that soldiers had taken his oxen.

Three families disputed ownership of the same house.

A child died in the southern hospital.

A messenger brought word that an allied captain had stopped a group of drunken soldiers from setting fire to a shop. Vibhishana sent his thanks and asked that the captain's name be recorded.

Another messenger reported that an allied patrol had beaten two men for refusing to repeat a victory chant.

Vibhishana ordered an inquiry.

The officer receiving the order asked whether the king wished to make a formal complaint against the allied forces.

Vibhishana looked at him.

“I wish the beating to stop.”

“That is not the same procedure, Majesty.”

“Then find a procedure that will do it.”

By evening he had signed forty-seven tablets.

He had read perhaps twice that many.

He had forgotten to eat until a servant brought food and stood beside him until he took a mouthful.

He had not seen Taranisena since morning.

He had not asked about Arun and Analā since the boatman's account.

The knowledge came to him suddenly, in the middle of a discussion about a broken sluice.

For a moment he could not hear the man speaking.

His children were missing.

He was discussing water.

The sluice mattered. Without it, three streets would flood when the next rain came.

He knew that.

He hated that he knew it.

“Majesty?” the engineer asked.

Vibhishana looked up.

“Show me where the wall has failed.”

The engineer pointed.

Vibhishana listened.

He gave instructions.

He signed the tablet.

When the man had gone, he sat for a long time with his hands on the table.

***

Taranisena was awake when Vibhishana came to his room.

The lamp was burning.

His son sat on the floor beside the bed.

“Did you eat?” Vibhishana asked.

“Yes.”

“Did you sleep?”

“No.”

Vibhishana lowered himself to the floor.

For a while they sat together.

Then Taranisena said, “I heard someone outside.”

“A servant.”

“He was running.”

“People run in palaces.”

“They didn't used to.”

Vibhishana had no answer.

His son leaned against him.

“Did you find Arun?”

“No.”

“Analā?”

“No.”

“Will you?”

Vibhishana looked towards the lamp.

“I will keep looking.”

Taranisena pressed his face into his father's shoulder.

Vibhishana held him.

He thought of the boatman who had seen a woman at the harbour. He thought of the child found beneath the stairway, the names copied twice, the bodies buried without names. He thought of his wife, whose death he knew, and of the two children whose deaths he did not.

He had imagined that becoming king would allow him to do something.

Now he had a throne and a palace and servants who bowed when he entered a room.

He could not make a clerk find a missing tablet.

He could not make a witness remember a name.

He could not send a patrol through his own city without asking another man's permission.

He could not tell his son that his brother and sister were alive.

At the door a servant appeared.

“Majesty. I'm sorry.”

Vibhishana did not turn.

“The northern sluice,” the servant said. “The wall has given way.”

Taranisena tightened his grip.

Vibhishana closed his eyes.

Then he kissed the top of his son's head.

“I have to go.”

“I know,” Taranisena whispered.

Vibhishana stood.

He went to the door.

Behind him, his son drew his knees up against his chest and watched the lamp.

The king walked into the corridor, where messengers were already waiting.

There were people in the flooded streets.

There were children who would need carrying.

There were names that would be entered into another register before morning.

He went to find them.

Across the sea, his daughter was asleep beneath a roof he had never seen.

Her name was written carefully in a book he did not know existed.

And tomorrow, when she woke, she would wait for him again.
