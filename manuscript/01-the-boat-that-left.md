# Chapter One

## The Boat That Left

The boy beside her had stopped crying. That frightened her more than the screaming.

His lips were blue, and a thin thread of seawater ran from his nose to his upper lip. Every time the boat rose on a wave, his head struck the wooden plank behind him with a small, hollow sound. She put her hand between his head and the wood. The next wave drove his skull against her knuckles. She did not move them.

The sea was black except where the burning city coloured it orange. Smoke lay so thick upon the water that the boats appeared and disappeared inside it, sometimes close enough to touch, sometimes gone entirely. Somewhere ahead, a woman was calling for a child named Nila. She had been calling for so long that the name no longer sounded like a name. It sounded like something breaking.

The boat smelled of fish, wet rope, vomit and the sharp, unfamiliar stink of burning oil. A little girl had been sick into her own lap and was trying to hide it beneath her hands. The boatman shouted at everyone to sit still. Nobody had stood up. He shouted again anyway.

Above them, something enormous moved through the smoke.

For a moment she thought a piece of the city had torn itself free and was falling into the sea.

Then the shape caught the moonlight.

Wings.

Not the wings of any bird she knew. They were too straight, too wide, and something beneath them flashed with a pale yellow light.

The children screamed.

The boatman looked up, and for the first time his voice failed him.

The thing turned slowly above the water.

One light.

One light.

Two.

Far ahead, another boat answered with a lantern.

The boatman bent over his oars.

“Keep your heads down.”

She kept hers up.

Her father would know what it was.

He knew the names of things that frightened other people.

***

Four years earlier, she had asked him whether the sea could swallow the moon.

They were sitting on the western terrace after supper. She was ten, and her feet did not yet reach the floor from the stone bench. Her father had placed a cushion beneath them, although she had insisted she was quite comfortable without it.

The moon lay across the water in a long, trembling path.

“If the sea is deep enough,” she had said, “why doesn't the moon fall in?”

“Because it is very well behaved.”

She had looked at him.

“The moon?”

“The sea. It knows what belongs to it.”

She considered this.

“Does it ever forget?”

“Frequently. That is why we have floods.”

He had been reading a document when she interrupted him. He folded it carefully and put it aside.

She remembered that about him.

He always put things aside.

Not immediately. Sometimes she had to ask twice, or pull at his sleeve, or climb into his lap and sit directly upon whatever he was reading.

But eventually he would look at her as though the entire world had been waiting patiently for her to speak.

“Can the sea take people?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Even good people?”

He was quiet for a moment.

“Yes.”

“Then how do they come back?”

“Sometimes they don't.”

She disliked the answer.

He saw it and drew her closer.

“But there are people who know the water. They learn its moods. They watch the wind. They build boats strong enough to carry others through it.”

“Are you one of those people?”

“No. I become seasick.”

She laughed.

He touched the tip of her nose.

“But I know how to find them.”

She had believed this completely.

She still did.

***

A wave struck the boat from the side.

Cold water poured over the gunwale and filled the space beneath her knees. The boy beside her slid sideways. She caught him by the shoulder, but his wet tunic slipped through her fingers.

“Hold him!” someone shouted.

“I am!”

The boat tilted again.

A man reached across the children and pulled the boy upright. He was missing two fingernails, and blood had dried in the creases of his palm. He pressed the boy against his chest.

“Is he yours?”

She shook her head.

“Where is his mother?”

She did not know.

The man looked at the boy, then at the city behind them.

He said nothing.

She tried to remember when the boy had begun crying. It seemed important. If she could remember that, perhaps she could remember who had brought him aboard.

But everything before the boat had become confused.

There had been a corridor.

A woman pulling her wrist.

A stone staircase with blood on the third step.

Someone had told her not to look behind her.

She had looked.

The roofs were burning.

Not all of them. She remembered that distinctly. Some roofs were still dark, and some windows were lit with ordinary lamps, as though people inside were preparing for bed.

That had frightened her more than the flames.

How could anyone be preparing for bed?

She had wanted to go home.

She had said so.

The woman holding her wrist had pulled harder.

She had asked where her father was.

Nobody answered.

***

Two years earlier, her father had returned home with a cut above his eyebrow.

It was not a serious cut. He told her this three times.

She did not believe him.

Blood had dried near his temple, and his hair smelled of rain and horses. He sat in the courtyard while a servant brought warm water.

She fetched a clean cloth herself.

“Who hurt you?”

“A door.”

She frowned.

“Doors don't hurt people.”

“This one was exceptionally determined.”

She climbed onto the bench beside him.

“Did you hurt it back?”

“I considered it.”

“Why didn't you?”

“It was a very old door.”

She pressed the cloth against his forehead.

He winced.

“Does it hurt?”

“A little.”

“Then why are you smiling?”

“Because you look very serious.”

She pressed harder.

He stopped smiling.

She remembered feeling pleased.

Later, when the wound had been cleaned and the servant had gone, he showed her how to fold the cloth so that the clean part remained against the skin.

“You mustn't keep touching the wound,” he said.

“Why?”

“Because it needs time.”

“How much?”

“That depends on how deep it is.”

She examined the small red line above his eyebrow.

“Will it disappear?”

“Probably.”

“And if it doesn't?”

“Then I shall have a scar.”

She thought about this.

“Will I remember it?”

“You may.”

“Will you?”

He took the cloth from her hand and folded it once more.

“I think you will remind me.”

She had.

For months afterwards, whenever he returned from a journey, she inspected his forehead.

Even after the cut had vanished.

Even when he laughed and told her she was being ridiculous.

She always checked.

***

The boat struck something beneath the water.

The sound was dreadful.

Wood scraped against stone. The children lurched forward together. Someone screamed that the bottom had broken.

The boatman stood.

“Sit down!” the man holding the blue-lipped boy shouted.

The boatman ignored him and pushed his oar into the water.

They had entered a narrow passage between rocks.

She could see them now, black and shining, rising on either side of the boat like the backs of enormous animals.

The current pulled them sideways.

The boatman shouted for another oar.

There wasn't one.

A second boat appeared through the smoke, close enough for her to see the faces of its passengers.

A little boy stood near the front, holding a wooden horse.

She recognised the horse.

For one impossible moment her heart leapt.

Her youngest brother had a horse like that.

Its left ear was broken.

She stood.

“Arun!”

The boatman seized her arm.

“Sit!”

“Arun!”

The other boat moved past them.

The boy turned.

He was not her brother.

His horse had two ears.

She sat down slowly.

The man holding the sick boy looked at her.

“Who is Arun?”

“My brother.”

“Is he on another boat?”

She nodded.

She did not know why.

The man waited for her to say more.

She looked away.

***

Eight days earlier, her father had not come to supper.

This was unusual, but not alarming.

He often worked late.

Sometimes he returned after the lamps had been extinguished, carrying the smell of ink and the cold night air into the house.

She had learned to recognise his footsteps.

Other men walked through the courtyard as though the stones belonged to them.

Her father walked quietly.

He always paused beside the old neem tree because one of its roots had lifted a paving stone.

She could hear that pause from her bedroom.

Then the soft sound of his sandals.

Then the door.

That evening, she waited for the pause.

It did not come.

Her mother ate very little.

The servants moved carefully around the table.

Nobody explained why.

She asked whether her father was at the palace.

Her mother said yes.

She asked whether he would be home tomorrow.

Her mother said she did not know.

She asked whether something had happened.

Her mother looked at her for a long time.

Then she said, “Finish your supper.”

The next morning, his travelling chest was gone.

So were his sandals.

His writing desk had been cleared of several documents, but the little clay elephant she had made for him remained beside the lamp.

Its trunk had broken during firing.

He had kept it anyway.

She touched the elephant.

The room smelled of sandalwood, dry paper and the faint medicinal oil he rubbed into his wrists when they ached.

She stood there for a long time.

Then she went to find her mother.

“Where has Father gone?”

“Across the water.”

“Why?”

“He has work to do.”

“When is he coming back?”

Her mother closed her eyes.

She opened them again.

“I don't know.”

The girl considered this.

Her father had crossed the water before.

He had visited ports and courts and distant settlements whose names she could never remember.

Sometimes he brought gifts.

Once he had brought a shell so large she could hear the sea inside it.

Another time he brought a little copper bell that made almost no sound.

She had loved the bell best.

“Then I will wait,” she said.

Her mother turned away.

***

The flying craft returned.

It passed lower this time, and she saw the dark shape of a person beneath the wings.

There were two smaller figures beside him.

Children.

One appeared to be lying down.

The craft shuddered.

Its left wing dipped sharply.

The boatman stopped rowing.

For several heartbeats, the machine seemed to hang motionless above the black water.

Then it rose.

A few children began to clap.

The boatman shouted at them to stop.

But she understood why they had done it.

Something had nearly fallen.

And it hadn't.

She looked towards the burning shore.

The city was smaller now.

Its towers had become dark teeth against the fire.

One of the western buildings collapsed, sending a fountain of sparks into the sky.

She knew that building.

There had been a fountain in its courtyard.

Her father had once taken her there to watch the engineers repair a waterwheel.

He had explained how the turning wheel could lift water higher than the place from which it came.

She had not understood.

He had drawn a picture in the dust.

She had understood the picture.

She wondered whether the waterwheel was burning.

Then she wondered whether her father was standing beside it.

The thought comforted her.

He would know what to do.

He always knew what to do.

***

When she was very small, she had been afraid of thunder.

Not lightning.

Lightning was beautiful.

Thunder was different.

Thunder entered the house without permission. It shook the doors and made the lamps tremble.

One night she had run into her father's room while rain hammered against the roof.

He was awake, reading.

She climbed into his bed without asking.

He moved the book aside.

“Is the sky angry?”

“No.”

“Then why is it shouting?”

“Because it is very large.”

She thought this was a poor explanation.

He seemed to agree.

He put the book down.

“Would you like to know a secret?”

She nodded.

“Thunder cannot see you.”

She pulled the blanket up to her chin.

“Are you sure?”

“Quite sure.”

“Then why is it so loud?”

“Because it doesn't know where you are.”

She considered this.

Another crash shook the shutters.

She buried her face against his chest.

His shirt smelled of sandalwood and the faint smoke of the evening lamps.

His hand rested upon her hair.

She could hear his heart.

It was steady.

The thunder continued.

But it sounded farther away.

After a while she asked, “Will you always be here?”

He did not answer immediately.

She lifted her head.

“Father?”

He looked down at her.

“I will always try.”

She frowned.

“That isn't the same.”

“No.”

She waited.

He brushed a strand of hair away from her forehead.

“But if you are frightened, you must remember that being frightened does not mean you are alone.”

She did not like that answer either.

She wanted a promise.

He seemed to understand.

He drew her close again.

“Sleep,” he whispered. “I am here now.”

And because he was there, she slept.

***

The boy beside her began to cough.

It was a weak, wet sound.

The man holding him turned his head sideways and rubbed his back.

Water came from the boy's mouth.

He coughed again.

Someone said he would live.

Nobody knew.

But the words moved through the boat like warmth.

She put her hand against the boy's shoulder.

He was trembling.

“What's your name?” she asked.

He looked at her.

His eyes were enormous.

“What's your name?”

His lips moved.

She leaned closer.

“Again.”

“Dev.”

“Dev,” she repeated.

He closed his eyes.

She said his name once more, very softly, so she would remember it.

***

They had been on the water for what felt like an entire night when the first boat reached the far shore.

She did not see it land.

She heard the shouting.

Then the lanterns.

Then, from somewhere beyond the smoke, three bright flashes.

The boatman began to cry.

He continued rowing.

She had never seen a grown man cry while working.

It made her uneasy.

The man holding Dev told her that they were nearly safe.

She looked at the shore.

There were trees.

Dark rocks.

A few lights moving between them.

No palace.

No walls.

No familiar rooftops.

She could not imagine living there.

But she did not need to.

They would only remain until her father came.

He would find a way to bring them home.

Or perhaps he would build them a new one.

He knew people who could do such things.

He knew engineers and boatmen and men who understood tides.

He knew how to speak to strangers.

He knew how to make frightened people listen.

He knew how to find the people who knew the water.

She remembered that.

She smiled.

Only a little.

***

As the boat turned towards the shore, she looked back one final time.

High above the burning city, a solitary figure stood upon the western cliff.

He was very small at that distance.

The smoke moved between them.

For a moment she could see him clearly enough to recognise the dark shape of his cloak.

Her uncle.

He was watching the boats.

She wanted to wave.

She raised her hand.

But the boat turned again, and the cliff disappeared.

She lowered it.

Her uncle would tell her father where she had gone.

Of course he would.

They were brothers.

Whatever had happened between them, surely they would not forget her.

***

The boat scraped against sand.

Hands reached from the shore.

Children were lifted out one by one.

Dev was carried past her, wrapped in someone's dry cloak.

She watched until he disappeared among the trees.

Then a woman reached for her.

“Come, little one.”

She shook her head.

“Come. You're safe now.”

“I have to wait.”

“For whom?”

“My father.”

The woman looked towards the sea.

“Is he on another boat?”

“No.”

“Then where is he?”

She pointed towards the burning city.

The woman followed her hand.

For a moment neither spoke.

Then the woman said, “We must get you warm.”

“He'll come.”

“Perhaps.”

“He will.”

The woman knelt before her.

She was young, with a burn across one cheek and salt dried white upon her eyebrows.

“What's your name?”

The girl opened her mouth.

But she was suddenly too tired to answer.

Her legs were shaking.

Her fingers had swollen in the cold.

There was a blister across her palm where the rope had cut it.

The woman took her hand.

“It's all right. You can tell me later.”

She led her towards a fire sheltered between two rocks.

Someone placed a blanket around her shoulders.

Someone gave her warm water in a clay cup.

It tasted of smoke.

She drank it anyway.

All around her, children were asking questions.

Where was their mother?

Where was their brother?

Had the other boats arrived?

Was the city still burning?

Would they be going home tomorrow?

She listened.

She knew the answer to only one question.

Her father would come.

***

She fell asleep sitting upright.

When she woke, the sky was beginning to pale.

The sea had changed colour.

It was no longer black.

It was grey, with a thin line of silver where the sun would rise.

A woman was singing somewhere behind her.

Not a song she recognised.

The words were gentle, but the melody made her think of people walking away.

She stood.

Her blanket fell to the sand.

The boats lay along the shore.

Some were overturned.

Others had been dragged beyond the tide.

People moved among them, counting children.

A man called out names from a tablet.

Each time someone answered, he made a mark.

She listened carefully.

Her brothers' names were not called.

She waited.

Perhaps they were on another tablet.

Perhaps they had arrived at a different beach.

Perhaps her father had already found them.

She walked towards the water.

The sea was calm.

It seemed impossible that it had been so violent only hours before.

She stood where the small waves reached her feet.

The sand was cold.

Behind her, someone called for her to return.

She did not turn.

Across the water, the city was hidden beneath a long, dark cloud.

She imagined her father waking in his room.

He would notice that she was gone.

He would ask the servants.

He would find her empty bed.

He would see the little copper bell beside her pillow.

He would know she had been frightened.

He would come.

She imagined him stepping from a boat, his sandals wet, his hair untidy from the wind.

He would see her waiting.

He would smile.

Not because anything was funny.

Because he had found her.

She would run to him.

She would be angry first.

She would ask why he had taken so long.

He would try to explain.

She would refuse to listen.

Then he would hold her.

And everything would be all right.

She could see it so clearly that she almost turned towards the sound of his footsteps.

Almost.

Instead, she stood at the water's edge.

Waiting.

The sun rose.

Her father did not come.
