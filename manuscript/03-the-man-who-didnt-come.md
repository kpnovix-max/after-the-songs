# Chapter Three

## The Man Who Didn't Come

*There are many ways to lose someone.*

The first ship came on a morning when Analā had almost stopped looking for one.

Not stopped waiting. That was different.

Waiting was what she did when she carried water from the well and glanced towards the strip of sea visible between two roofs. Waiting was what she did when Ilā talked too quickly and she missed half the words because a gull had cried above the harbour. Waiting was what she did at night, when the baby breathed against Mēni's shoulder and the house became quiet enough for her to imagine footsteps outside.

Looking required a particular place, and time, and the willingness to be disappointed.

She had become rather good at disappointment.

That morning, Ilā was showing her how to separate the fibres of a coconut husk. Analā had managed to produce a small pile of brown dust and a blister.

“You're pulling the wrong way,” Ilā said.

“You said to pull.”

“I said to pull *apart*.”

“They are apart.”

“That's dust.”

“It was very difficult dust.”

Ilā opened her mouth to reply, but a bell began ringing from the shore.

It rang three times, stopped, then rang without any apparent intention of stopping again.

Ilā was on her feet.

“A ship.”

Analā stood so quickly that the husk rolled into the ashes.

For a moment she could not speak. Then she asked, “From Lanka?”

“Where else?”

There were several possible answers to that question. Analā did not give any of them.

She ran.

***

For nearly a month, the island had been making room for people who were not supposed to stay.

Children slept in lofts, beside cooking fires, beneath tables. A house that had once accommodated four now held seven. The western sheds were full. The island's goats had become unusually accustomed to being pursued by children who wanted to know whether they would bite.

The children from the boats had begun to lose their sameness.

On the first morning they had all been wet, hungry, frightened and unmistakably foreign. Now some wore island clothes. Some had learned the paths through the palms. Others had acquired the same brown knees and sunburnt noses as the children who had always lived there.

An older boy from Lanka had spent three days arguing that the island's method of fishing was wrong. On the fourth, he caught nothing. On the fifth, he began asking questions.

Two sisters had refused to sleep apart. They now quarrelled so fiercely over a sleeping mat that their host mother threatened to give them separate rooms, although she had no separate rooms to give.

And Ilā had begun introducing Analā as her sister whenever it was useful.

“She is my sister,” she told the woman who sold sweet cakes.

“You told me last week she was your cousin.”

“That was before.”

“Before what?”

“Before we became sisters.”

The woman gave them one cake between them.

Ilā said this proved the arrangement was working.

Sēnan had other concerns.

Each evening he sat with the household register and a small collection of stones. One stone for a household. Another for each child it had taken. He moved them about as though a different arrangement might somehow create more rice.

The royal tablet remained wrapped in cloth in his house.

It promised provisions.

It promised a ship.

It promised the children would be returned when the fighting ended.

The island had received none of the three.

He sent men to watch the approaches to the reef. Twice, fishermen returned with rumours of smoke on the mainland. Once, a trader from the northern coast claimed that Ravana's army had driven the invaders into the sea. He had heard it from a fisherman who had heard it from another vessel. The following day, another trader brought a different story: Ravana was dead. Neither man had been to Lanka. Neither knew who governed it now.

Sēnan believed neither.

He had learned that news travelled fastest when nobody knew whether it was true.

Analā came to his house every few days.

“Has anyone heard from my father?”

“No.”

“Did the traders know his name?”

“I asked.”

“And?”

“They had heard several names.”

“Was his one of them?”

“No.”

She always thanked him.

That was the worst part.

***

The bell was still ringing when Analā reached the shore.

People were already gathering on the sand. A few children cheered. Someone had begun calling for the recorders. An old woman stood with both hands pressed to her mouth, staring at the horizon as though she had been waiting for this particular vessel all her life.

Analā pushed between two fishermen.

The ship was coming in from the east.

It was broad and low in the water, its deck stacked with barrels and sacks beneath a patched awning. Not a warship. Not one of the elegant vessels Analā remembered in the harbour at home. Its hull was stained and its railings were crowded with things that had been tied down badly.

There were flags.

At first she was pleased by them. A ship carrying official banners meant someone had sent it. Someone had remembered.

Then she saw the colour.

Saffron cloth hung from the mast, the stern and two long poles lashed to the rails. One banner had come loose at the bottom and slapped against the wood with each movement of the ship.

A drum sounded across the water.

Then another.

A voice shouted something. Several voices answered.

“Jai Shri Ram!”

The words came again, louder, with the drumbeats stumbling behind them.

Analā had heard Rama's name before. In the palace it had been spoken in worried voices, then in angry ones, then not at all when she entered a room. She knew it belonged to the man whose army had come against her uncle.

She had never heard anyone sing it.

The singing rolled over the water in rough, triumphant waves. One man struck a drum so hard that its loose skin rattled. Another beat the ship's railing with a wooden cup.

A sailor leaned over the side and vomited.

Someone applauded him.

Ilā appeared beside Analā, breathless.

“Is that how they come to fetch people in Lanka?”

“No,” Analā said.

She had answered before she meant to.

“No,” she said again.

***

Sēnan arrived in a clean tunic with two men carrying the royal tablet.

He stopped at the edge of the water.

There was no herald. No signal requesting permission to land. No officer standing at attention while the vessel came through the reef. The sailors were shouting instructions over the music and then laughing when nobody obeyed them.

He watched the ship's prow swing dangerously close to a fishing canoe.

“Move it!” someone aboard yelled.

The canoe's owner hauled it aside.

A rope was thrown. It struck an islander across the shoulder. The man caught it anyway.

Then the gangplank came down.

Six men came ashore from the ship, though at first it was difficult to tell which of them was in command. More sailors stayed aboard to mind the cargo, the ropes and the vessel itself.

The loudest was a broad-shouldered man in a saffron shirt that strained across his stomach. A sword hung from a belt decorated with small brass discs. He carried a short spear in one hand, not because he needed it to walk, but because he seemed to enjoy pointing with it.

A thick saffron mark ran from the bridge of his nose towards his hairline. It was slightly crooked.

His eyes were bright and restless.

He stepped onto the sand, spread his arms, and smiled at the islanders as though they had arranged a celebration for him.

“Brothers!”

Nobody answered.

“Brothers!” he shouted again. “Where are your smiles? Has the sea taken them?”

One of the sailors laughed too loudly.

The man in saffron laughed with him. Then, without warning, he stopped.

“Who is in charge here?”

Sēnan stepped forward.

“I am Sēnan. Welcome to our island. May I ask whom I have the honour of receiving?”

The officer stared at him.

For several seconds he said nothing.

Then he turned towards the ship.

“Did you hear that?”

The sailors grinned.

“He wants to know who I am.”

Sēnan kept his hands folded.

“We are accustomed to receiving the seal of the authority that sends a vessel. It is our practice.”

The officer looked down at his shirt, then up at the flags.

“Can't you see my flag?”

“Yes, sir. But—”

“But?”

His smile disappeared.

Sēnan felt the men beside him become very still.

The officer came close enough that Sēnan could smell fermented palm liquor on his breath.

“Have you forgotten who won?”

Sēnan hesitated.

“We have heard different accounts, sir.”

The officer stared at him.

“Different accounts?”

“The news has been uncertain.”

The officer turned towards the ship.

“Hear that? They haven't even heard!”

The sailors laughed.

“Rama has won,” the officer said. “Remember it.”

Sēnan lowered his head.

“Yes, sir.”

“Then why are you asking me questions?”

A quieter voice came from behind him.

“He has a right to see the orders, sir.”

An older sailor had stepped down from the gangplank. His hair was grey at the temples, his hands scarred with old rope burns. He wore a plain working tunic and carried a rolled document beneath one arm.

He spoke as someone might speak to a difficult child without wishing the child to notice.

“The tablet, sir. It'll save us time.”

The officer's expression changed at once.

“Of course! The tablet!”

He slapped the older sailor's shoulder, hard enough to make him shift his footing.

“See? We have tablets. We have seals. We have everything.”

The older sailor unrolled the document. Sēnan saw a Lankan seal beneath the name of a senior official he recognised.

The orders authorised the recovery of the children evacuated from Lanka.

The number written there was three hundred and nine.

Sēnan felt relief.

Someone had remembered.

The officer leaned close again.

“Are you satisfied?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Good.”

He turned to the sailors.

“Music!”

***

Years earlier, Sēnan had stood on this same beach waiting for a different visitor.

He had been younger then, and more frightened of making mistakes.

Ravana's vessel had arrived at dawn with its sails furled neatly and its crew lined along the rail. A herald had stepped ashore first, announced the king's presence, and waited while Sēnan completed the customary welcome.

Sēnan had prepared a speech.

It was a long speech. He had worked on it for three evenings.

Ravana listened to the first part, then glanced towards the harbour wall.

“Does that hold in the western wind?”

Sēnan lost his place.

“The wall, Majesty?”

“Yes.”

“Not always.”

“Then why are you telling me the island prospers?”

Sēnan had looked down at his speech.

Ravana's mouth twitched.

“Show me.”

They walked to the harbour. An elderly woman was repairing a net beside the boats. Ravana stopped to ask why she was using two kinds of cord.

“The good cord comes from Lanka,” she told him. “The other is what we have when it doesn't.”

“Which breaks first?”

“The one that is cheaper, Majesty.”

“Then it isn't cheaper.”

The woman looked at him for a moment, then laughed.

Ravana laughed too.

He stayed longer than planned. He asked about storms, grain stores, the shallow channel where vessels sometimes grounded, and how many days the island could feed itself if the fishing failed.

When Sēnan tried to thank him for his attention, Ravana waved it away.

“Thank me when the rope arrives.”

The rope arrived before the next monsoon.

Sēnan had remembered that more clearly than anything in his prepared speech.

Now, on the same sand, a man in saffron was demanding music.

***

The drumbeat began with a hard, hollow thump.

The officer lifted his spear above his head and shouted the chant again. A sailor answered him. Another sailor, red-faced and sweating, came down from the ship with a second drum.

They began to dance.

At first it was only stamping. A foot driven into the sand, a shoulder jerked towards the sky, a turn that nearly knocked a barrel from the gangplank.

Then the officer caught the red-faced sailor by the wrist.

He pulled him close.

For a moment they stood almost chest to chest, knees bent, eyes fixed on one another.

The drummer found a slower beat.

Thum.

Thum-thum.

The two men began to circle.

Their arms stretched out around each other's bodies, wide and curved, as though they were embracing something too large to hold. Their hands never quite touched. Their hips rolled towards each other and away again, the movement absurdly careful for men who had spent the morning stumbling over ropes.

The officer's face changed.

His smile vanished. His eyes opened wide, dark and intent, and he stared into the other man's face with such ferocity that Analā, watching from behind a stack of fishing baskets, thought they were about to fight.

The sailor stared back.

Their feet slid sideways.

Their hips swung.

The officer leaned forward, his mouth tightening, his gaze fixed as though he meant to swallow the man whole.

Then, exactly on the turn of the drum, both men closed their eyes.

Their faces softened into enormous smiles.

The officer tipped his head back, lips parted, wearing the dreamy expression of someone who had forgotten every care in the world. The other sailor smiled too, almost tenderly.

They turned again.

Eyes open.

The smiles disappeared.

Their faces became fierce, hungry, solemn.

Another turn.

Eyes closed.

Bliss.

Again.

The rhythm was slow enough that everyone on the beach could see the change coming, and strange enough that nobody could tell what it meant.

They circled each other with their arms outspread, hips thrusting and withdrawing, the brass discs on the officer's belt chiming against his sword. Their shoulders rose and fell in elaborate little shudders. The officer pursed his lips and shook his head with the solemn concentration of a priest performing a sacred duty.

The drummer struck harder.

The men bent their knees almost to the sand, rose together, and rolled their hips in a long, ridiculous sweep. They were grown men, heavy and armed, playing at something childish with the intensity of men deciding another person's fate.

A sailor on the ship clapped in time.

One of the island boys gave a nervous laugh.

His mother caught his arm so sharply that he stopped.

The officer heard the laugh.

He turned his head without breaking the dance.

His eyes opened.

He looked directly towards the boy.

The boy shrank behind his mother.

Then the officer closed his eyes, smiled beatifically, and continued circling.

Analā could not look away.

The dance was foolish. She knew that. Ilā had performed sillier dances in Mēni's courtyard and made the baby laugh until he hiccupped.

But nobody here was laughing freely.

The officer's smile seemed capable of becoming something else before the next drumbeat.

He swung suddenly towards his partner and caught him around the neck.

The drummer faltered.

The officer drew his sword halfway from its sheath.

The blade flashed beside the sailor's cheek.

The sailor's smile stayed where it was. His eyes did not.

For one suspended moment the two men stood locked together, the sword between them.

Then the officer threw back his head and roared with laughter.

He kissed the sailor's forehead.

“Again!”

The drum began again.

The sailor danced.

His mouth was smiling.

His hands trembled.

***

The violence that followed began so casually that Sēnan did not at first understand it was beginning.

A sailor was carrying a coil of rope from the ship. He was lean and dark-haired, with the cautious movements of someone who knew exactly how much space he was permitted to occupy.

The officer called him over.

“Come, brother. Why aren't you dancing?”

The man shifted the rope against his shoulder.

“I'm working, sir.”

“Working!” The officer looked around in delight. “He is working while the rest of us celebrate.”

One of the younger sailors said, “He doesn't celebrate our victories.”

“Why not?”

The young man grinned.

“Ask him what he is.”

The officer's smile widened.

“Tell our friends. What are you?”

The sailor said nothing.

The officer waited.

“Rakshasa,” the younger man supplied.

A few islanders exchanged glances.

The officer nodded slowly, as though he had learned something fascinating.

“Ah.”

He stepped closer.

“Then say it.”

The sailor kept his eyes lowered.

“Say what, sir?”

The officer's voice was suddenly gentle.

“You know what.”

The drummer tapped once against the rim of his instrument.

“Jai Shri Ram,” the officer said.

The sailor's fingers tightened around the rope.

“I have work to do.”

The slap sounded like a plank striking wet stone.

Analā flinched behind the baskets.

The sailor staggered. Before he could recover, the younger man spat at his feet and then at his chest.

“Say it.”

The older sailor took one step forward.

“We'll need him to bring us through the reef on the way out, sir.”

The officer looked at him.

“Will we?”

“Yes, sir. The channel shifts.”

The officer considered this, then smiled.

“Then we must take care of our precious guide.”

He tapped the sailor's cheek with the flat of his spear.

“Say it.”

The man did not.

The officer's smile disappeared.

“Bind him.”

Two men took the rope from his shoulder and twisted it around his wrists. He resisted just enough to remain standing.

One kicked him in the stomach.

He folded forward with a sound that was more breath than voice.

Sēnan heard a child begin to cry.

The officer looked towards the sound.

“Why are you frightened?”

Nobody answered.

“Why is everyone frightened today?”

He sounded genuinely puzzled.

Then he laughed.

“Rama has won. You should be happy.”

Analā pressed her back against the rough wood of the baskets.

Her uncle's officers had sometimes frightened her. She had seen men punished in the palace courtyard. She knew that power could be cruel.

But this was different.

The men were laughing.

They were singing.

And the man they had struck was still expected to help sail their ship.

***

Sēnan had intended to bring all the children to the shore.

He had instructed the household recorders to prepare for their departure. Families were packing bundles. Mothers who had spent weeks complaining about the extra mouths were crying over children they had barely known a month.

Now the officer turned towards him.

“How many?”

Sēnan blinked.

“Sir?”

“Children. How many children?”

The tablet said three hundred and nine.

Sēnan knew it. He had counted them himself. He knew the households, the names, the ages, the communities. He knew which little girl had a fever and which brothers must not be separated.

He looked at the bound sailor.

The man was trying to stand straight.

“How many of them are Rakshasas?” the officer asked.

Sēnan swallowed.

“Why do you ask, sir?”

The officer's eyes widened.

“Why?”

He turned to the others.

“He wants to know why!”

The sailors laughed.

The officer leaned towards Sēnan and spoke in a low, confidential voice.

“We'll treat them like kings.”

The laughter grew louder.

Sēnan felt the sand shift beneath his feet.

He had no plan. There was no time to make one.

“A hundred…” he began.

The officer waited.

“A hundred and three.”

The officer stopped laughing.

“What?”

“A hundred and three children, sir. That's how many reached us.”

Sēnan could hear his own heartbeat.

He had meant to say a different number. He knew that much. The figures had slipped and collided in his head while the officer was smiling at him.

But the number was spoken now.

The officer looked towards the older sailor.

“Read it.”

The older sailor unrolled the tablet again.

“Three hundred and nine embarked, according to the record.”

The officer turned back.

“Three hundred and nine.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And you have?”

“A hundred and three.”

“Where are the others?”

Sēnan's mouth had gone dry.

“We received only those who reached the island. The others—”

“The others what?”

“We don't know where they went.”

The officer's expression became very serious.

“Do you think I am a fool?”

“No, sir.”

“Do you think Lord Rama is a fool?”

“No.”

“Then swear it.”

Sēnan said nothing.

“Swear by Rama's name.”

A sailor snorted. Another laughed.

The officer took a step closer.

“Why do you look frightened?”

He reached towards Sēnan's face.

Sēnan's shoulders jerked before he could stop them.

The officer paused, delighted.

“Oh! You thought I was going to hit you.”

He dipped his thumb into the saffron paste on his own forehead and drew a thick mark across Sēnan's brow.

“There. Now Rama is with you.”

The officer's thumb smelled of sweat and liquor.

Sēnan did not move.

The officer's smile faded.

“Bring me the children.”

***

Sēnan turned to his recorders.

He spoke in the island language, quickly and quietly.

“Bring those listed under the other communities. Not the Rakshasa children. Do not call anyone from the houses until you check the entries. Keep the others away from the shore.”

The nearest recorder stared at him.

“Sēnan—”

“Go.”

The man went.

Sēnan had no idea whether the order would be obeyed, whether the officer had understood, or whether any child would cry out at the wrong moment.

He only knew that the man with the sword must not see the Rakshasa children.

Analā saw the recorders hurry up the path.

She had heard Sēnan's words. She understood enough of the island language now to know that the Rakshasa children were to remain hidden.

She did not understand why he had said a hundred and three.

She did understand why he had said *hide*.

Ilā found her behind the baskets.

“Come,” she whispered.

“Where?”

“Inside. Mēni says inside.”

Analā looked once more towards the ship.

A month earlier she would have run towards any vessel from Lanka, even if she had to swim the last stretch.

Now she crouched lower.

She followed Ilā.

From the doorway of a storage hut above the beach, she could still see the gangplank.

***

The children arrived in frightened little groups.

Some carried bundles. Others had been summoned so quickly that they came barefoot and empty-handed. A girl kept asking where her brother was. An older boy told her he would come on the next ship, although nobody had told him that.

The recorders counted.

Then counted again.

One hundred and three.

Ten non-Rakshasa children remained in households across the island, overlooked in the haste and confusion. Sēnan would discover the mistake later. At that moment he was too frightened to ask whether the number was correct.

The officer watched the children approach.

“Are any of these Rakshasas?”

Sēnan shook his head.

“We have no reliable way to tell, sir.”

The older sailor glanced at him.

Sēnan could not meet his eyes.

“You don't record their communities?” the officer asked.

“Not in a way that would help you, sir.”

It was not a good lie.

Sēnan knew it.

The officer looked as though he knew it too.

Then a small boy began crying for his sister.

“She's still up there,” he said, pointing towards the houses. “She came with me. She was on our boat.”

The officer turned sharply.

“What did he say?”

The boy stopped speaking.

The older sailor crouched beside him.

“What's your name?”

The boy whispered it.

“Your sister's staying with a family?”

The boy nodded.

“We'll tell them to send her on the next ship,” the older sailor said.

“Will there be another?”

The sailor hesitated.

“There are always more ships.”

He stood and guided the boy towards the gangplank.

Sēnan watched him.

He had seen the older sailor stop the officer from striking the bound man again. He had seen him speak gently to the child. He had seen him take the tablet without being asked and keep it out of the officer's hands while the man danced.

The older sailor came to him.

“We'll take these children home,” he said quietly.

Sēnan glanced towards the officer.

“Are you certain?”

“I know the route. I'll see them delivered.”

“And the others?”

The older sailor's expression did not change.

“You say they never arrived.”

Sēnan said nothing.

“Then that's what I'll write.”

“You believe me?”

The sailor looked past him at the children.

“I believe I have a hundred and three children to get home.”

He had children of his own in Lanka. Three of them. His eldest was nearly grown, and he had been saving for a house away from the harbour, where the younger ones might sleep without waking to soldiers shouting in the streets.

He did not tell Sēnan any of this.

He merely adjusted the tablet under his arm and said, “Let us finish before he changes his mind.”

***

The officer had found a small brass bowl of drinking water.

He emptied it onto the sand, demanded arrack, and was told the islanders had none prepared.

His face darkened.

Then he smiled.

“Never mind. We brought our own.”

The sailors cheered.

A drum began again.

The officer turned to Sēnan.

“One hundred and three.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Not three hundred and nine.”

“No, sir.”

“Do you know what happens to men who lie to Rama?”

Sēnan could not answer.

The officer stepped close, drawing his sword.

The blade was short and broad, polished unevenly. A nick near its tip caught the light.

He lifted it towards Sēnan's head.

Behind Sēnan, someone gasped.

The officer rested the flat of the blade lightly against his hair.

For a moment he was absolutely still.

Then he said, almost affectionately, “Why are you frightened?”

The drum stopped.

Sēnan could smell the arrack.

The officer's eyes searched his face.

Then he smiled so widely that his cheeks folded.

“Jai Shri Ram!”

The sailors shouted it back.

The drums crashed into life.

The officer removed his sword and turned away as though nothing had happened.

The older sailor took the tablet to the ship.

He entered the number.

One hundred and three recovered.

Two hundred and six unaccounted for, reported lost before reaching the island.

A clerk in Lanka would later copy those words into another register. Someone else would copy them again. With each copy, the distinction between *reported lost* and *lost* would become less important.

For now, they were only marks on a tablet.

***

The children climbed aboard.

Some were excited. Some were crying. Several looked back towards the paths that led inland.

The older sailor helped a girl over the gangplank and lifted a smaller boy by the waist.

The bound Rakshasa sailor had been untied enough to work. He stood near the stern, one hand pressed against his stomach.

When the officer passed him, the man lowered his eyes.

The officer patted his cheek.

“Good fellow.”

The sailor did not answer.

The ship's ropes were cast off.

The drums continued until the vessel cleared the shallows.

From the storage hut, Analā watched the saffron flags move out across the water.

Ilā sat beside her without speaking.

Below them, Mēni stood among the islanders, holding her baby so tightly that he began to complain.

The ship had brought sacks of grain, though fewer than Sēnan had hoped. It had taken a hundred and three children.

It had also taken something that could not be counted.

For weeks Analā had imagined a ship coming from Lanka. She had imagined the colour of its sails, the sound of its ropes, the moment someone would call her name.

She had imagined her father stepping ashore.

She had imagined being angry with him.

She had saved whole speeches for the occasion.

Now she watched the vessel grow smaller.

The men aboard were still singing.

It seemed impossible that this was a ship from her home.

The harbour she remembered had been full of rules. A sailor bowed before addressing an officer. The palace guards stood straight even when they were tired. Her uncle's seal meant that someone was answerable for what happened beneath it.

She had never thought much about those things.

Now she wanted them back with an ache she could hardly bear.

The saffron flags disappeared beyond the reef.

The sound of the drums remained a little longer.

Then that too was gone.

***

That evening Sēnan sat alone with the register.

The household names were still there. Beside each were the names of children, written carefully by men who had believed the writing was temporary.

He counted those remaining.

Two hundred and six.

He counted again.

Something was wrong.

He turned back to the first page.

“Why a hundred and three?” he murmured.

The recorder sitting opposite him looked up.

“Sir?”

“The rest were Rakshasas.”

The recorder shook his head.

“No, sir.”

Sēnan looked at him.

“There are two Nishada children still here. Five Vanara. Three Yaksha.”

Sēnan stared at the register.

“Ten?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Why weren't they brought?”

The recorder hesitated.

“You said a hundred and three.”

Sēnan remembered the officer's sword resting against his head.

He remembered the drums.

He looked down at the names.

Ten children who could have gone home.

Ten children whose families might be waiting for them.

He had not chosen to keep them.

He had simply been afraid.

For a long time neither man spoke.

Then Sēnan closed the register.

Outside, the islanders were arguing about the grain.

They had expected provisions for three hundred and nine children. The ship had left supplies calculated for a little more than a hundred. The remaining mouths would have to be fed from stores meant for the island's own families.

An old fisherman came to the doorway.

“We can't do this for long.”

Sēnan looked at him.

“How long?”

“I don't know.”

“Then we will do it until you know.”

The fisherman did not smile.

“Ravana is dead, they say.”

Sēnan looked towards the wrapped tablet.

He remembered the king on the harbour wall, asking about the western wind. He remembered the rope arriving before the monsoon.

“Yes,” he said.

The fisherman waited.

Sēnan closed the register.

“The children are not.”

***

Mēni found Analā sitting outside the house after dark.

The baby had finally gone to sleep. Ilā was inside, pretending not to listen.

“Were you hoping to go?” Mēni asked.

Analā looked towards the sea.

“Yes.”

“And now?”

Analā rubbed at the blister on her palm. It had hardened since the morning.

She thought of the man in saffron, his eyes closing in pleasure as he danced. She thought of the same eyes opening again, hungry and bright. She thought of the sailor folding around the kick, and of the officer asking everyone why they were frightened.

She had never seen Rama.

She knew that.

But when she tried to imagine him, she saw the man on the beach.

She hated that she did.

“I don't want that ship,” she said.

“No.”

“I don't want another one like it.”

Mēni sat beside her.

After a while Analā said, “Father wouldn't come on a ship like that.”

Mēni did not answer.

“He would know,” Analā said.

She did not know what she meant. He would know the men were cruel. He would know she was afraid. He would know which ship to take, which harbour to enter, how to find her even if nobody had written her name down.

He had always known.

“I think I'll wait for him,” she said.

Mēni put an arm around her shoulders.

Inside, Ilā shifted on her mat.

Far beyond the reef, the sea was dark again.

Analā listened for oars.

Not drums.

Oars.

Her father had not come.

She would wait for him.
