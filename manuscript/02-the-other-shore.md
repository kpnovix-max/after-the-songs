# Chapter Two

## The Other Shore

*The sea had delivered them. It had promised nothing else.*

The first thing Analā noticed was that the woman had no servants.

The second was that the woman did not seem to mind.

She was sitting outside a small house, her feet planted in the dust, peeling something pale and knotted with a knife. A baby slept against her hip, held there by a length of faded cloth. Beside her, a pot was beginning to boil over a fire made from three blackened stones.

The woman reached for a piece of wood, pushed it farther into the flames, caught the pot before it spilled, and continued peeling.

All without waking the baby.

Analā watched from the shade of a tree.

The woman looked up.

“Are you hungry?”

Analā nodded.

“Then come here.”

She went.

The woman pointed at a shallow basket.

“Take those out.”

Analā looked into it. There were perhaps twenty of the pale, knotted things, still covered in earth.

“Where?”

“Here.”

The woman indicated a flat stone beside her.

Analā lifted one carefully between her fingers.

It was damp and gritty. A little soil fell onto her foot.

“Like this?”

The woman looked at her.

“Have you never held a root before?”

Analā considered the question.

She had seen roots. She had eaten them, probably. There had been a dish at home with a thick yellow sauce and something soft inside it, which her father liked and she did not. She had once asked the cook what it was made from and had forgotten the answer.

“No,” she said.

The woman waited.

Analā placed the root on the stone.

“Is there someone who prepares them?”

For a moment, the woman simply stared.

Then she laughed.

It was a loud, sudden laugh, and the baby woke with a furious little cry.

“Oh, now look what you’ve done.”

Analā stepped back.

The woman bounced the baby, who was red-faced and indignant.

“I prepare them,” she said. “And I cook them. And I wash the pot afterwards, unless my husband remembers he has two hands.”

She said this without bitterness.

Analā looked towards the house.

It had one door and two small openings for windows. Bundles of dried leaves hung from the roof. A fishing net lay across a wooden frame, its broken strands tied together with little knots. Inside, she could see a rolled sleeping mat and several clay vessels.

There was nowhere for the servants to sleep.

She realised this before she could stop herself from looking for their quarters.

The woman noticed.

“What’s your name?”

“Analā.”

“I’m Mēni.”

Analā inclined her head slightly.

It was the sort of movement she had been taught to make when greeting an older woman whose position was uncertain.

Mēni looked amused.

“That’s a very grand way to say hello.”

Analā lowered her eyes.

“I’m sorry.”

“What for?”

She didn’t know.

Mēni handed her another root.

“Here. Hold it firmly. It won’t bite.”

Analā took it.

Behind them, the sea moved against the stones with a soft, persistent sound, like someone washing a great quantity of cloth.

She had never noticed how much noise water could make when nobody was shouting over it.

---

The island had looked smaller from the sea.

From the boats, it had been a dark strip beneath the morning sky, with a cluster of trees and a pale line where the waves broke.

Now Analā could see that the shore curved away in both directions, disappearing behind low hills covered in thick vegetation. Paths ran between houses built from timber, clay and woven palm. There were gardens enclosed by crooked fences, narrow boats resting on wooden supports, and long racks of fish drying in the sun.

The smell was everywhere.

Salt. Wet rope. Smoke. Fish. Something sweet fermenting in a clay jar.

And beneath it all, the warm, sour smell of people living close together.

At home, the air had smelled of sandalwood, polished floors and flowers brought fresh each morning. Even the kitchens had been separated from the living rooms by courtyards and passages. She had known when food was being prepared because someone told her.

Here, everything happened in front of everybody.

A man was washing his own clothes in a basin.

An old woman was cutting a boy’s hair with a pair of small metal blades.

Two girls were arguing over a broken water pot.

Someone was singing while scraping scales from a fish.

Nothing seemed to have a proper place.

And yet everyone appeared to know exactly what to do.

Except her.

---

The islanders had been waiting for the boats.

This surprised Analā.

She had imagined that they would have to explain themselves, that someone would ask where they had come from, and that she would be required to speak on behalf of the children.

Instead, before the first boat had been dragged above the tide, men had come down the beach carrying water and baskets of fruit.

They had counted the boats.

Then they had begun counting the children.

That count could not be finished with the first arrivals. Ten boats had made the island crossing; three others had turned into the southern caves when smoke closed the channel. Through the morning, the islanders watched for them. One came before noon, another in the early afternoon, and the last was towed around the headland by two island fishing boats. The children aboard were frightened and hungry, but alive.

The flying craft had reached the eastern shore ahead of the boats. Its landing had broken one of the pilot's arms. Islanders had carried her to a house to be treated, and had brought the two feverish children who travelled with her into the shade. They were counted with the others. The pilot was not asked to fly again.

Only when the last cave boat had unloaded could Sēnan's recorders finish their work. There were three hundred and nine children altogether, including the two from the craft.

The counting took a very long time.


The counting was only the beginning.

Beneath a broad-leaved tree, two men sat with writing tablets balanced across their knees. One called the children forward. The other recorded their names.

“Name?”

“Age?”

“Community?”

The questions were repeated so often that the children began answering before they were asked.

“Nishada,” said a boy who had been holding his younger sister's hand since they came ashore.

“Vanara,” said another, though he was uncertain whether he was eleven or twelve.

“Rakshasa,” said a girl whose name the recorder had already asked her to repeat twice.

The man wrote each answer without looking up.

Analā waited her turn.

She gave her name, her age, and her community.

The recorder made three marks beside her name.

Then he called the next child.

Nobody seemed particularly interested in the answers. They were simply things that needed to be written down.

Some of the smaller children were frightened of the islanders and would not stand still. Others wandered away to look at the trees or the crabs moving sideways across the sand. One boy insisted he had already been counted twice. A girl began crying because she could not find her brother, who was sitting directly behind her.

Eventually, a man with a narrow face and a white cloth tied around his head called for silence.

He did not receive it.

He waited.

Then he climbed onto an overturned fishing boat.

That helped.

“My name is Sēnan,” he said. “I speak for the households of this shore.”

Someone asked whether there was food.

“Yes.”

Someone else asked whether they could go home.

Sēnan looked towards the sea.

“Not today.”

Analā remembered those words.

Not today.

It sounded reasonable.

It sounded like something an adult would say when a child asked a question whose answer was already understood.

She had heard her father say it often.

Not today, Analā.

Not while the council is sitting.

Not until you’ve finished your lesson.

And once, when she had asked to accompany him to the northern garrisons:

Not today, little bird. The roads are not as interesting as you imagine.

He had brought her a painted wooden bird when he returned.

Its wings were fixed to a small turning wheel. When she pushed it along the floor, they moved up and down.

She wondered whether it was still in her room.

---

The tablet was brought ashore wrapped in two layers of oilcloth.

It had travelled in the boat nearest the front, guarded by a palace attendant whose name Analā did not know.

The attendant was an older man with a badly swollen ankle. He had been helped across the beach by two islanders and was now sitting on a low stool beneath a tree.

When Sēnan approached, the man tried to stand.

“Stay seated,” Sēnan said.

The attendant ignored him.

He drew the tablet from its wrapping and held it out with both hands.

Analā recognised the seal immediately.

It was the royal seal of Lanka.

Not the one used on ordinary household correspondence. This was the heavier impression reserved for proclamations issued in the king’s name.

She moved closer.

The tablet was dark, its edges polished by handling. The letters had been cut deeply enough to survive salt and rain.

Sēnan examined it.

Then he looked towards the children.

“All of them?”

The attendant nodded.

“As many as could be brought.”

“How many?”

“Three hundred and nine, according to the last count.”

Sēnan’s face changed.

It was not fear exactly.

It was the expression of a man who had been told the cost of something he had already agreed to purchase.

He read the tablet slowly.

Analā knew the formal language. She had copied enough proclamations during her lessons to recognise its careful repetitions, its insistence on titles, its peculiar way of making even simple requests sound as though they had been discussed by a hundred people.

The children were to be received under the protection of the island’s households.

They were to be fed and sheltered until the cessation of hostilities.

Lanka would provide grain, oil, cloth and other necessities. The islanders would be compensated for what they supplied.

Arrangements for the children’s return would follow.

There was a date.

There was the seal.

And there was the name of the chamberlain.

Analā felt something loosen inside her.

The chamberlain knew where they were.

The king knew where they were.

Her uncle had sent them here.

It was not an accident. It was not a flight into nowhere.

Someone had made arrangements.

She looked out at the sea.

Her father would find out.

Of course he would.

---

The discussion beneath the tree continued long after the tablet had been read.

Analā stood close enough to hear most of it.

Sēnan wanted to know when the first provisions would arrive.

The attendant said they had been promised with the next supply vessel.

“How soon?”

“I cannot say.”

“How long are we expected to keep them?”

“Until the fighting ends.”

“And when will that be?”

The attendant’s face was grey with exhaustion.

“I cannot say that either.”

Sēnan looked at him for a long moment.

Then he folded the oilcloth around the tablet.

“We will do what we agreed to do.”

He turned to the people gathered behind him.

“Every household that can take children will take them. The storehouse will be opened. The western sheds can be cleared before night.”


By afternoon, the names had been arranged beneath the names of households.

Some families took one child. Others took three or four. The larger houses took as many as they could fit, and several children were sent along the paths that disappeared beyond the hills, to settlements Analā had not seen from the shore.

Each household was given the names of the children entrusted to it.

The recorders marked where every child had gone.

The arrangements took most of the day.

Children were called forward, given bundles of clothing or something to eat, and led away by people whose names they immediately forgot.

Brothers and sisters protested when they were separated. Sēnan made the recorders change their entries so that they could remain together.

Other children attached themselves to whoever happened to be standing nearby.

By evening, the paths were crowded with children carrying unfamiliar possessions towards unfamiliar homes.

And already, there was confusion.

A boy from the boats followed two island children into the wrong house and was discovered eating supper there.

A little girl insisted that the child sleeping beside her had travelled from Lanka, though the child's mother had given birth to him on the island.

An island boy claimed to have arrived on the largest boat, until his grandmother reminded him that he had spent that morning helping her mend a fishing net.

The adults laughed.

The children argued.

And by the following morning, most had stopped asking who had come from where.

They were simply the children living in the houses.

The men and women who had brought the children across were not among the households asked to take them in. They had served as rowers, pilots, guards and physician's assistants on an evacuation, not as settlers. Some had heard scraps of news before the boats left, but none had a reliable account of the final battle; the people at the oars had been occupied with keeping children alive.

The crews left in stages, taking their boats to safer harbours to seek relatives or help others still at sea. Sēnan gave them water and what provisions he could spare. The injured palace attendant was carried aboard with his ankle bound. The pilot of the flying craft, her broken arm splinted by an island healer, travelled with the last crew rather than risk another flight. By the end of the second day, the last evacuation vessel had gone. No one from the Lankan evacuation party remained as an island resident.

In the confusion Sēnan did not ask the crews about the identity of any particular child. When he learned Analā’s father’s name later that day, he was occupied with placing the children; he had no reason to suppose the boatmen knew more about her than she did. The islanders were now responsible for the children and had only occasional trading vessels to tell them what was happening across the water.

A woman asked what would happen when the stores ran out.

Sēnan said they would discuss it after counting what they had.

A fisherman protested that the boats were already returning with poor catches.

Sēnan told him the sea had not become less generous merely because there were more mouths.

The fisherman replied that the sea had never been generous in the first place.

Several people laughed.

Analā did not.

She was still thinking about the tablet.

Its language had been reassuring.

But the attendant had looked frightened when Sēnan asked how long the children would stay.

---

Sēnan noticed her when the households began choosing whom they could accommodate.

She was standing beside two younger children who had fallen asleep against each other.

He looked at her clothing.

It had once been fine.

The fabric was torn at one shoulder, and the lower edge was stiff with salt. There was a dark stain across one sleeve that she had tried to wash away in the sea.

But the embroidery remained.

So did the small gold clasp at her throat.

Sēnan’s eyes rested on it.

“Whose daughter are you?”

Analā straightened.

“My father's name is Vibhishana.”

Sēnan waited.

She added, quietly, “He is the king's brother.”

Sēnan looked at her again.

This time, more carefully.

“King Ravana?”

“My uncle.”

The people nearest them stopped speaking.

Analā was accustomed to that.

At home, mentioning her uncle often produced a particular sort of silence. Adults would stand a little straighter. They would choose their words more carefully.

She had never liked it very much.

But here, surrounded by strangers, she was relieved to hear the silence.

It was something familiar.

Sēnan bowed his head.

Not deeply.

Enough.

“Then you are very far from home, my lady.”

“Yes.”

“Were you sent with the others?”

Analā hesitated.

“My mother brought me to the boats.”

“Was that arranged beforehand?”

“No.”

Sēnan glanced towards the attendant sitting beneath the tree.

“Does he know you are here?”

“I don't think so.”

“Then your name won't be among the others.”

Analā shook her head.

Sēnan considered this for a moment.

“Is your mother here?”

“No.”

“Will she follow?”

“She said she would.”

Sēnan glanced towards the sea.

Analā saw the glance.

She had begun to dislike that particular movement of people's eyes.

Adults did it whenever she mentioned someone who had not arrived.

They looked towards the sea as though it might answer for them.

“Do you know when?” he asked.

Analā shook her head.

Sēnan did not ask anything else.

He turned to the recorder and asked him to add her father's name beside her entry. It was information for finding a family, not an announcement to the island. The recorder made the mark and went back to his work.

He called a woman over and spoke to her quietly.

Analā caught only a few words.

“…the royal household…”

“…somewhere suitable…”

“…until arrangements…”

The woman looked at Analā, then at the children sleeping against her.

She nodded.

Analā waited for someone to tell her where to go.

---

They gave her a place in Mēni’s house.

There were already three children there, two from the boats and one who belonged to the household.

The youngest was a boy of perhaps six who had lost one sandal and was deeply concerned about it.

Mēni’s daughter, a girl called Ilā, was eleven.

She had dark hair tied into a thick knot and a small scar beside her left eyebrow. She moved quickly, spoke without waiting to be asked, and seemed to possess a remarkable ability to carry several objects at once.

She showed Analā where to sleep.

“Here.”

Analā looked at the mat.

It was made from woven leaves, with a folded cloth at one end.

“Is there another room?”

Ilā shook her head.

“For whom?”

Analā looked towards Mēni.

“For your mother and father.”

“They sleep there.”

“Where?”

Ilā pointed to the other side of the same room.

Analā was silent.

Ilā watched her.

“Have you never slept in one room with your parents?”

“No.”

“Why?”

Analā tried to think of an answer that would not sound rude.

“They have their own rooms.”

“Both of them?”

“Yes.”

Ilā considered this.

“Do they quarrel?”

Analā looked up.

“No.”

“Then why?”

It was such a reasonable question that Analā could not answer it.

Ilā grinned.

“My father snores. Mother says she’d put him outside if the goats would have him.”

Analā almost smiled.

Almost.

---

By the following morning, Analā had learned that the island had rules.

They were not written on tablets.

Nobody announced them.

And people became impatient when she did not know them.

Water was fetched before the sun grew hot.

Cooking fires were shared.

The cleanest water was kept for drinking.

The smaller children ate first.

Fish had to be covered before the flies found it.

Doors were not locked during the day.

Everyone swept the space outside their house.

Everyone.

On the second morning, Ilā handed Analā a broom.

It was made from stiff twigs bound with fibre.

Analā held it uncertainly.

“What is this for?”

Ilā looked at her.

“Sweeping.”

“I know.”

“Then sweep.”

Analā glanced at the ground.

It was made of earth.

She could see small leaves, fish scales and pale grains of sand scattered across it.

“Where should I put everything?”

Ilā pointed towards a shallow pit beyond the house.

Analā began.

The broom caught in the uneven ground. Dust rose around her ankles. She pushed too hard and broke one of the twigs.

Ilā watched for a while.

Then she took the broom, demonstrated three quick movements, and handed it back.

“Like that.”

Analā tried again.

A little later, Mēni came outside.

She surveyed the ground.

Then she looked at Ilā.

“Why does it look worse?”

Ilā burst out laughing.

Analā stared at the broom.

Mēni looked from one girl to the other.

“Oh,” she said.

Her expression softened.

“Never mind. The dust was here before us. It can wait another morning.”

Analā wished she had not said that.

She would have preferred to be scolded.

---

At home, she had been good at things.

She could read both the formal script used in royal records and the quicker hand used by merchants.

She knew the names of the rivers that fed the northern plains.

She could recite long passages from the histories her tutor insisted she memorise.

She understood why the harbour taxes changed with the seasons, though she had once argued with her father that the calculations were unnecessarily complicated.

He had listened to her entire argument.

Then he had asked whether she had remembered to account for the ships that arrived empty.

She had not.

He had smiled.

“Then perhaps the calculations are complicated for a reason.”

She had been furious with him for nearly an hour.

Later, he had found her in the garden and asked whether she had discovered where the empty ships went.

She had spent the rest of the evening explaining it to him.

He had pretended to be astonished.

Here, none of those things mattered.

Ilā knew how to keep a fire burning in the rain.

She could clean a fish without wasting the flesh.

She could carry a water jar on her hip and another in her hand.

She knew which leaves soothed insect bites and which ones made the skin burn.

Analā could do none of these things.

When Ilā discovered that Analā had never fetched water, she stared at her with open admiration.

“Never?”

“No.”

“Not even once?”

Analā shook her head.

Ilā considered this astonishing fact.

Then she said, “You must have been terribly thirsty when everyone was busy.”

Analā laughed.

It escaped before she could stop it.

A small sound.

But it startled her.

Ilā smiled, pleased with herself.

Analā looked away.

For a moment, she had forgotten to be frightened.

Then she remembered.

And the feeling returned so quickly that she could almost taste it.

---

The nights were the worst.

During the day, there was always something to do or someone asking a question.

At night, the house became a collection of unfamiliar sounds.

The roof creaked when the wind moved through the palms.

The baby breathed in little wet whistles.

Mēni’s husband snored, stopped, and began again.

Somewhere beyond the wall, an insect made a steady clicking sound that seemed to come from inside Analā’s ear.

The woven mat scratched the skin behind her knees.

She could smell the cooking fire in her hair.

She had washed her face, but there was still salt beneath her fingernails.

At home, someone would have brought warm water.

Someone would have combed her hair.

Someone would have shaken out the bedding and drawn the curtains.

Her mother sometimes came to see whether she had fallen asleep.

Her father came less often, because he worked late.

But when he did, he always found something to say.

Once, he had stood in the doorway and announced that a very serious matter had arisen in the council.

She had sat up immediately.

“What happened?”

“A dispute over the ownership of three mango trees.”

She had stared at him.

“Three?”

“Three.”

“Couldn’t they count them?”

“Apparently, everyone could count them. That was the difficulty.”

He had explained the dispute for so long that she had fallen asleep before hearing how it ended.

The next morning, she had demanded to know who won.

He had said the mangoes did.

She had thrown a cushion at him.

Now, lying on the mat, Analā pressed her face into the folded cloth.

It smelled of smoke and somebody else’s soap.

She tried to remember the exact sound of her father’s laughter.

She could remember his face.

She could remember the way he raised one eyebrow when she said something outrageous.

She could remember the weight of his hand resting on her head.

But the laughter would not come.

Only the memory that there had been laughter.

She lay very still.

On the other side of the room, Ilā turned over in her sleep.

Analā wanted to ask whether there were ships that travelled between the island and Lanka.

She wanted to ask how long such a journey took.

She wanted to ask whether the islanders would recognise her father’s ship when it came.

But Ilā was asleep.

And Analā had already learned that questions sounded different here.

At home, someone had always been ready with an answer.

On the island, people often said they did not know.

And then continued with whatever they were doing.

---

On the third day, the first rain came.

It arrived without warning.

One moment, the air was thick and hot. The next, the trees were bending, the sky had darkened, and water was striking the roofs so hard that Analā could not hear Mēni calling from the doorway.

Ilā ran outside to collect the drying cloth.

Analā followed.

Within moments, her hair was soaked.

Water ran down her neck and beneath her collar.

The ground softened under her feet.

Ilā snatched a cloth from the line and thrust it into her hands.

“Take that inside!”

Analā obeyed.

Then she returned for another.

And another.

The rain made it difficult to see.

They were both laughing by the time the last cloth was brought indoors.

Mēni stood in the doorway, shaking her head.

“Two clever girls, and neither thought to bring the basket.”

Ilā laughed harder.

Analā smiled.

Then she saw the sea.

Beyond the houses, through a gap between the trees, the water had turned almost black.

Rain struck its surface in countless tiny explosions.

The horizon had disappeared.

There was nothing beyond it.

No Lanka.

No ships.

No familiar coastline.

Nothing.

The smile left her face.

Mēni noticed.

“Cold?”

Analā shook her head.

“Come inside anyway.”

She followed.

That evening, Mēni warmed a cloth near the fire and handed it to her.

Analā accepted it.

“Thank you.”

Mēni sat beside her.

“You’ve been very quiet.”

Analā looked down.

“Were you always this quiet at home?”

She shook her head.

“No?”

Another shake.

Mēni waited.

Analā folded the warm cloth carefully across her knees.

“My father says I talk too much.”

Mēni smiled.

“Does he?”

Analā nodded.

“He says I ask questions faster than people can answer them.”

“That sounds like a useful talent.”

Analā looked at her.

For the first time, she seemed to consider saying more.

Then she lowered her eyes.

Mēni did not press her.

She reached for the basket of roots and began peeling.

After a while, Analā picked one up.

She held it firmly, as she had been shown.

Mēni watched without comment.

---

The first argument about the children happened on the fourth evening.

Analā did not hear all of it.

She was sitting with Ilā near the western storehouse, helping separate a tangled length of fishing line, when voices rose from the meeting place.

A man was saying that the grain would not last.

Another said the children were not eating any more than children usually ate.

The first man replied that this was precisely the problem: there were now three hundred and nine more children than there had been before.

Someone mentioned the tablet.

Someone else asked when Lanka would send the promised supplies.

Nobody knew.

Analā listened.

The fishing line slipped between her fingers.

Ilā pulled it back.

“Not that end.”

Analā tried again.

The voices continued.

A woman said they could manage until the next harvest.

A man asked what would happen if the harvest failed.

Sēnan said they would discuss the harvest when there was a harvest to discuss.

The argument became louder.

Then Mēni’s voice cut through it.

“Has anyone asked the children whether they’d like to starve?”

There was a pause.

“Because if not, perhaps we can stop discussing it in front of them.”

Analā looked towards the meeting place.

Mēni was standing with her hands on her hips.

Someone muttered something.

She answered sharply.

The people nearest her laughed.

The argument moved indoors.

Ilā bent over the fishing line again.

“Mother always wins when she’s angry.”

Analā looked at her.

“Does she?”

“Usually.”

“My father doesn’t like people raising their voices.”

Ilā shrugged.

“Then he’d have a terrible time here.”

Analā thought about that.

Then, unexpectedly, she smiled.

---

That night, she asked Mēni about the tablet.

“Will Lanka send the grain?”

Mēni was stirring a pot.

“They said they would.”

“Then they will.”

Mēni glanced at her.

“You sound certain.”

“My uncle doesn’t break promises.”

The stirring stopped.

Only for a moment.

Then Mēni continued.

“I hope you’re right.”

Analā watched the wooden spoon moving through the thickening food.

There were questions she wanted to ask.

Whether the island had received messages from Lanka before.

Whether ships could sail during the rains.

Whether someone would tell her father where she was.

Instead, she said, “The chamberlain signed it.”

“The tablet?”

“Yes.”

“Do you know him?”

Analā nodded.

“He used to complain that my uncle gave him too much work.”

Mēni smiled.

“Perhaps he did.”

“He always said it when my uncle wasn’t there.”

“That sounds sensible.”

Analā looked at her.

Mēni’s expression was perfectly serious.

Analā smiled again.

It was becoming easier to do that when she wasn’t thinking.

And harder to bear afterwards.

---

The days began to acquire a shape.

In the mornings, Ilā took her to fetch water.

At first, Analā could carry only a small vessel without spilling it.

The larger ones bruised her hip.

Her hands developed tender places where the handles rubbed.

Once, she dropped a pot and broke it.

She stood staring at the pieces, horrified.

Ilā looked at the fragments.

Then at Analā.

“Well,” she said, “now you can carry two smaller ones.”

Analā did not understand.

Ilā pointed at the broken halves.

“Two.”

Analā laughed so hard that she had to sit down.

Later, when she offered to replace the pot, she realised she had nothing with which to pay for it.

Her gold clasp was still at her throat.

For the first time, she considered what it might be worth.

She had never had to consider the value of anything she wore.

Mēni refused to take it.

“It’s a pot,” she said.

“I broke it.”

“Yes.”

“I should replace it.”

“You can help make another.”

Analā looked at her.

“Make one?”

Mēni nodded.

“Unless your family has someone who does that too.”

Analā opened her mouth.

Then closed it.

Mēni laughed.

---

One afternoon, Sēnan came to the house.

He had brought the tablet.

Analā was sitting near the doorway, helping Ilā sort dried beans.

She stood when she saw him.

He inclined his head.

“My lady.”

Mēni looked up from her work.

Analā wished he had not said it.

Sēnan noticed.

“I was told you can read the formal script.”

“Yes.”

“There is a passage I would like you to explain.”

He unwrapped the tablet.

Analā knelt beside it.

The letters were familiar. She could almost hear her tutor’s voice correcting the pauses between clauses.

Sēnan pointed to a line concerning the provision of grain.

“Does this mean the supplies are to be sent immediately, or after the fighting ends?”

Analā read it twice.

“Immediately,” she said.

“Are you certain?”

She nodded.

“The return arrangements are after the fighting. The provisions are not.”

Sēnan studied the inscription.

“You read that very quickly.”

Analā looked down.

“My tutor made me practise.”

“Then your tutor did something useful.”

She did not know whether to smile.

Sēnan rolled the tablet in its cloth.

“Thank you.”

He was about to leave when Analā spoke.

“May I ask something?”

“Of course.”

“Will you send someone to Lanka?”

Sēnan paused.

“When the sea allows it.”

“Could you ask whether anyone has heard from my father?”

“Vibhishana?”

She nodded.

“Is he in Lanka?”

“No.”

Sēnan looked at her.

“He left before the fighting began.”

“Where did he go?”

Analā hesitated.

She had asked that question at home.

More than once.

Her mother had told her that her father had important matters to attend to, and that he would return when he could.

Analā had not liked the answer then.

She liked it even less now.

“I don't know,” she said.

“Perhaps someone in Lanka knows where he went.”

“Yes.”

“Then we'll have to find out.”

She bowed her head.

“Thank you.”

He left.

Analā remained standing in the doorway.

For the rest of the afternoon, she was unusually attentive to the path leading down to the shore.

---

On the sixth evening, the sea returned something.

A broken piece of timber washed onto the beach.

It was dark, polished on one side, and marked with a strip of faded red paint.

A fisherman found it among the stones and carried it to the meeting place.

Several children gathered around.

One said it came from a boat.

Another said it was part of a palace door.

A third insisted that he had seen the same red paint on the boats that had brought them from Lanka.

Analā examined it.

She could not tell.

The wood smelled of salt and old tar.

A little green weed clung to one end.

The fisherman turned it over.

“Could be from anywhere.”

But the children continued discussing it.

By the time the evening meal was served, someone had said that another boat from Lanka had been destroyed.

Someone else said the sea was full of wreckage.

A boy claimed that an entire fleet had burned.

Analā listened.

She remembered the red paint.

She remembered the fisherman saying it could be from anywhere.

She said nothing.

---

That night, the wind changed.

It came from the direction of Lanka, warm and heavy with rain.

Analā sat outside Mēni’s house after the others had gone to sleep.

The ground was cool beneath her feet.

A small moth battered itself against the lamp beside the door.

Beyond the trees, the sea made its endless sound.

She had begun to recognise the different noises it made.

The sharp hiss when a wave broke over sand.

The hollow knocking of water beneath the fishing boats.

The low, rolling sound that came from farther out, where the reef lay hidden.

At home, she had never needed to listen so carefully.

She had always known where she was.

Mēni came outside carrying a folded cloth.

“You should sleep.”

Analā nodded.

But she did not move.

Mēni sat beside her.

For a while, neither spoke.

Then Mēni said, “You’re waiting.”

Analā looked towards the sea.

“Yes.”

“For your father?”

She nodded.

“Do you know when he’ll come?”

“No.”

Mēni drew the cloth around her own shoulders.

“Then you may have to be patient.”

Analā considered this.

She had never thought of herself as patient.

Her father certainly hadn’t.

He used to tell her that she expected the world to finish speaking before it had begun.

But she had been patient here.

She had waited through six mornings.

She had carried water.

She had learned to sweep.

She had slept on a mat that scratched her skin.

She had eaten food whose names she did not know.

She had washed her own clothes and discovered that wet cloth was much heavier than dry cloth.

She had done all of it.

And nobody had told her how much longer she must wait.

“He’ll come,” she said.

Mēni turned towards her.

Analā’s voice was very soft.

“He always does.”

Mēni looked at the sea.

She did not say that she hoped so.

She did not say that Analā should prepare herself for disappointment.

She simply reached over and tucked the loose end of the cloth around the girl’s shoulders.

Analā let her.

For a moment, she thought of her mother standing beside the boats, telling her to go ahead.

I’ll come later.

She wondered whether her mother had found another way out of the city.

She wondered whether her brother was with her.

She wondered whether her father had returned home and found the house empty.

Perhaps he was angry.

He would be frightened first, of course.

But afterwards he would be angry that nobody had told him where she had gone.

She could imagine the questions.

Who authorised this?

Who was responsible for her?

Why wasn’t I informed?

And then, when he found her, he would probably pretend to be stern.

She would explain that she had been very brave.

He would say that bravery did not excuse disobedience.

She would tell him about the boats, the rain, the broken pot, and the woman who had no servants.

He would laugh at that.

She was certain he would laugh.

And she would tell him that she had learned to carry water.

He would ask her to demonstrate.

She would refuse.

He would insist.

She would tell him that a princess should not be made to carry water merely to entertain her father.

And he would say something ridiculous.

Something about princesses and water.

She could almost hear him.

Almost.

Mēni rose.

“Come inside, child.”

Analā stood.

Before entering, she turned once more towards the sea.

There was no light on the horizon.

No sail.

No sound except the waves.

But the tablet was safe in Sēnan's keeping.

The islanders knew who she was.

The chamberlain had made arrangements.

Someone would tell her father about the boats.

And somewhere, beyond the darkness, he was alive.

She did not know where.

She did not know what had happened to him.

She did not know that the world she had left was already becoming a place people would describe differently, depending on which shore they stood upon.

She knew only that her father had never failed to find her before.

Inside, Ilā was sleeping with one arm thrown across her face.

The baby made a small sound and settled again.

Analā lay down on the woven mat.

She pulled the unfamiliar cloth over her shoulders.

And for the first time since leaving Lanka, she fell asleep before the sea had gone quiet.

Far beyond the reef, the wind moved across the dark water.

It carried no message.

The islanders would learn to listen for one anyway.
